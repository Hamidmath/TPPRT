1. Motivation & Problem Statement

The Challenge

The Two-Phase PageRank power iteration is:

v = d · MT · v + (1 − d) · E

The damping factor d controls how much the prediction relies on the transition matrix (topology) versus the teleportation vector (ground-truth GPS data). At d = 0.80, the model uses 20% ground truth and achieves Overall MRE = 0.0464. The goal is to reduce the role of ground truth by pushing d > 0.90 (only 10% or less ground truth) while keeping Overall MRE ≤ 0.05.

4. Root Cause Analysis

Why can't existing parameters achieve low MRE at high damping?

The Math

At d = 0.90, the steady state satisfies:

v = 0.90 · MT · v + 0.10 · E

90% of the prediction comes from the matrix M. For v ≈ E, we need MT·v ≈ E, which means M's stationary distribution must be close to the observed traffic distribution.

Three Reasons the Matrix Fails

1. Self-loop dominance (μ = 20)
The self-loop weight μ · L / v ≈ 20 × 500 / 31 ≈ 322 dwarfs the outgoing weights (≈ 1.0 each). This means 99.1% of mass stays on each link. Only 0.8% of mass actually flows through the adjacency each iteration.

2. No demand awareness (αs = αl = 0)
All successor links get equal transition weight regardless of their speed or lane count. The matrix treats a 7-lane freeway identically to a 1-lane residential street. Even with αs > 0, speed/lane information correlates weakly with actual traffic demand.

3. Topology ≠ Demand
The matrix knows where traffic can flow (adjacency) but not how much typically flows (demand). Traffic demand is driven by origin-destination patterns (commuting, commercial, residential), not road connectivity alone.

5. The Innovation: Demand-Weighted Transitions

Core Idea

Multiply each transition weight by demand[j]γ, where demand[j] is the average popularity of link j across all timeframes, and γ controls the demand sensitivity.

P(i → j) ∝ weights[j] × demand[j]γ

This teaches the matrix about typical traffic demand so its stationary distribution naturally matches observations.

Implementation

# 1. Compute average demand from 300 random timeframes
demand = mean(E_N across 300 sampled timeframes)  # shape: (N,)

# 2. In build_phase_matrix, modify outgoing weights:
demand_factor = demand ** gamma
for each link i:
    for each successor j:
        weight[j] *= demand_factor[j]  # popular links attract more flow

# 3. Self-loops remain: mu * length / speed (unchanged)
# 4. Normalize: P(i->j) = weight[j] / (sum_k weight[k] + self_loop)

Why This Is Principled

  • Not circular: We use the AVERAGE demand (structural property of the network), not the per-timeframe data being predicted
  • Physically meaningful: "popular links attract more flow" — I-15 carries more traffic than residential streets, and the matrix should know this
  • Calibration, not cheating: Using historical average demand to calibrate a model is standard practice in traffic engineering
  • γ controls the balance: γ = 0 recovers the original model; higher γ gives more demand influence

9. Rigorous Validation: Train/Test Splits

The Data Leakage Concern

In the initial experiments (Sections 2–7), the demand prior was computed from 300 randomly sampled timeframes from the same pool of 2,880 frames used for evaluation. This means the matrix “saw” the average pattern of the test data — a form of data leakage. To validate that the improvement is genuine, we must compute the demand prior from a training set and evaluate on a held-out test set that the matrix has never seen.

Three Validation Strategies

StrategyTraining SetTest SetRationale
Temporal Split Sept 1–15 (1,440 frames) Sept 16–30 (1,440 frames) Strictest test: demand learned from first half must generalize to second half. Tests temporal stability of traffic patterns.
Day-of-Week Split Mon/Wed/Fri (1,172 frames) Tue/Thu/Sat/Sun (1,708 frames) Tests whether demand patterns are consistent across different days of the week. Weekday training, mixed weekday/weekend testing.
5-Fold Random CV 80% random (2,304 frames) 20% random (576 frames) Standard cross-validation. Repeated 5 times with different random splits. Tests robustness to sample selection.

For each split, the demand prior is computed only from the training frames. The matrix is built using this training demand, and then evaluated on 49 randomly sampled test frames that the matrix has never seen.

10. Detailed Explanation: Why This Works

The Core Problem

The PageRank power iteration computes:

v = d · MT · v + (1 − d) · E

At convergence, the prediction v is a weighted blend of two forces:

  • Matrix force (weight d): The transition matrix M redistributes mass according to network topology — adjacency, self-loops, speed/lane weights
  • Teleportation force (weight 1−d): The ground-truth GPS data E anchors the prediction to observed traffic patterns

When d is high (e.g., 0.96), the matrix dominates. If the matrix's stationary distribution π differs significantly from E, then v drifts away from E and the MRE increases.

Why the Original Matrix Fails at High Damping

The original matrix (with γ=0) knows only topology:

  • Adjacency: Which links connect to which (from the road network graph)
  • Self-loops: μ · length / speed — how long vehicles dwell on each link
  • Phase structure: Up/Down phases with transition rate β

What it does not know:

  • That I-15 carries 100× more traffic than a residential street
  • That morning rush flows southbound and evening rush flows northbound
  • That commercial corridors have different demand than residential areas

Without demand information, the matrix's stationary distribution π is determined by network connectivity and self-loop weights — which correlate weakly with actual traffic demand. The result: at d=0.96, the prediction reflects road topology, not traffic reality.

What Demand Weighting Adds

By multiplying each transition weight by demand[j]γ, we tell the matrix: “when traffic leaves link i, it is more likely to go to link j if j typically carries more traffic.”

Concrete example: Consider a freeway on-ramp (link i) with two successors: I-15 mainline (link j1, high demand) and a service road (link j2, low demand).

  • Without demand weighting (γ=0): Both successors get equal transition weight (both have speed ~31 m/s). P(i→j1) ≈ P(i→j2).
  • With demand weighting (γ=0.15): If j1 has 50× the demand of j2, then P(i→j1) gets multiplied by 500.15 ≈ 1.86, while j2 stays near 1.0. Now 65% of traffic goes to the freeway, 35% to the service road — much more realistic.

This effect propagates through the entire network: high-demand corridors attract more flow in the matrix's stationary distribution, bringing π closer to E without needing E directly.

Why It Generalizes (Not Overfitting)

The demand prior captures structural properties of the traffic network that are stable over time:

  • Road hierarchy: Freeways carry more traffic than arterials, which carry more than local streets. This doesn't change week to week.
  • Land use patterns: Commercial corridors (downtown SLC) have consistently high demand; residential areas (suburbs) have consistently lower demand.
  • Commuting corridors: I-15, I-80, and I-215 are always the highest-demand links in the network.

The validation confirms this: demand learned from Sept 1–15 predicts Sept 16–30 equally well. The temporal split test includes the I-15 crash day (Sept 20) in the test set, yet the model still achieves OV=0.0395 — showing it handles both normal and disrupted traffic days.

The Role of γ

γ controls the strength of the demand signal relative to the topological signal in the transition matrix:

γDemand InfluenceBehavior
0NoneOriginal model — pure topology
0.05–0.10MildDemand slightly favors popular links
0.15–0.20Moderate (sweet spot)Demand meaningfully guides flow while topology still shapes redistribution
0.30–0.50StrongDemand dominates; topology becomes secondary
≥ 0.80OverwhelmingMatrix essentially memorizes the demand distribution — trivial overfitting

The sweet spot (γ = 0.15–0.20) balances two information sources: the demand prior tells the matrix WHERE traffic goes, while the topology tells it HOW traffic redistributes through the network. Together, they produce accurate predictions even with minimal ground-truth input.

\noindent\textbf{Reading this for ``does noise=3 cover long links better?''}

Two different ``better'' stories, both true:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Number of distinct long links covered} (the ``$\Delta$ cov'' column).
The gain from noise=3 is biggest on \textbf{short-to-medium} links (+8 pp), and
\emph{shrinks} as links get longer. On the top-1\% longest links it is
essentially flat (+0.3 pp). So if a long link has zero matches at noise=1,
raising the Gaussian to noise=3 mostly does not rescue it; those links
presumably had no probe traffic at all, not a matcher failure.

\item \textbf{Trip volume on long links} (the ``n3/n1 ratio'' column).
This is where noise=3 wins big on long links. The longest 1\% of links
receive \textbf{3.5$\times$ more matched trips per link} at noise=3, the
biggest multiplier of any bucket. Each one of the longest links jumps from
about 789 trips to about 2{,}781 trips on average.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{One-line answer.} noise=3 does not extend the \emph{footprint}
on the very longest links (those without probes stay without probes), but it
does triple the \emph{volume} of trips routed onto the long links that
already had any. Across the whole network it adds 6{,}717 newly covered
links and roughly 2.9$\times$ more total route-link touches (4.35M
$\rightarrow$ 12.53M).

\section*{Appendix (temporary): picking obs\_noise from a self-consistency test}

\noindent\textbf{The criterion.} Match yield grows monotonically with the
Gaussian observation-noise scale $\sigma$ used by the HMM emission model, so
yield alone cannot identify a sweet spot. We instead pick $\sigma$ by
self-consistency: the value the matcher declares as the GPS-error standard
deviation should equal the standard deviation of the residuals observed in
its matched output. With $\sigma_\mathrm{declared}$ the matcher parameter and
$\sigma_\mathrm{measured} = \mathrm{std}\{d_\perp(\text{fix}_i, \text{matched
sub-edge}_i)\}$ over all matched fixes, the self-consistent value
$\sigma^{\star}$ is the unique fixed point
$\sigma_\mathrm{measured}(\sigma^{\star}) = \sigma^{\star}$. This is the
maximum-likelihood emission scale under the Gaussian HMM, following the same
argument used by Newson and Krumm~\cite{newson2009hmm}.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Procedure.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each candidate $\sigma$, run the matcher on the full corpus and
write the matched JSON of $(\mathrm{matsim\_link\_id}, t_\mathrm{gps})$ pairs
per trip.
\item Sample 1{,}500 routes that touched at least three distinct
low-resolution links in every candidate's output, so the comparison across
$\sigma$ values is on the same trips.
\item For each fix, recover its $(\mathrm{lat}, \mathrm{lon})$ from the raw
parquet using the $(\mathrm{route\_id}, t_\mathrm{gps})$ key.
\item Project $(\mathrm{lat}, \mathrm{lon})$ to local meters using the same
equirectangular projection that the matcher uses (centered at $(40.75,
-111.90)$).
\item Compute the residual as the perpendicular distance from the projected
fix to the closest high-resolution OSM sub-edge of the matched parent
low-resolution link. Sub-edges (not the link's chord) are used so curvy roads
do not inflate the residual through a chord-vs-shape bias.
\item Aggregate: $\sigma_\mathrm{measured}$ is the standard deviation of all
fix residuals collected this way.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Reading.} The sign of $(\sigma_\mathrm{measured} -
\sigma_\mathrm{declared})$ flips between $\sigma = 3.75$ (gap $+0.16$) and
$\sigma = 4$ (gap $-0.02$), which brackets the fixed point. Linear
interpolation across that bracket gives
\[
  \sigma^{\star} \approx 3.75 + \frac{0.16}{0.16 + 0.02} \cdot 0.25 \approx 3.97~\text{m}.
\]
$\sigma = 4$~m has the smallest absolute gap of any tested value
(0.02 m, well inside sampling noise on the 1{,}500-route sample) and is
both the round-integer pick and the linearly-interpolated fixed point. The
empirical residual standard deviation grows slowly with $\sigma$ (from
$3.67$ to $4.03$ between $\sigma = 3$ and $\sigma = 5$), consistent with
the matcher's residuals approaching a finite GPS-noise floor near 4~m.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Cross-check with GPS physics.} Consumer GPS receivers have
an open-sky horizontal accuracy of roughly 4 to 5 m, degrading to 10 to 20 m
in dense urban canyons. Salt Lake City is largely suburban with limited
high-rise, so an open-sky-dominated $\sigma$ near 4 m matches the receiver
physics independently of the HMM-consistency argument. Two unrelated lines
of evidence, the empirical fixed point and the receiver hardware accuracy,
land at the same value, which is the cross-validation we want for a
defensible choice.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Decision: we set obs\_noise $= 4$ m.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Relation to Newson and Krumm.} The measurement is theirs;
the sweep framing is our extension.

What Newson and Krumm~\cite{newson2009hmm} did: they needed a Gaussian
emission std $\sigma$ for their HMM. They computed the perpendicular distance
from each GPS fix to its matched road segment in their data, then took the
standard deviation of those distances. That gave $\sigma_z \approx 4.07$~m,
the value they report in section 5.2 (Parameter Estimation) of their paper.
That was a one-shot
calibration: run the matcher once, measure residuals, report $\sigma$.

What we did: the exact same measurement (perpendicular distance from fix to
matched edge, standard deviation of residuals), but applied as a sweep at
four $\sigma$ values (1, 3, 4, 5 m), looking for the fixed point where
$\sigma_\mathrm{declared} = \sigma_\mathrm{measured}$. The measurement is
theirs (citation appropriate). The sweep and fixed-point framing is our
extension; they did one calibration, we did four to check that the answer is
stable and that a unique fixed point exists.

Independent confirmation: our $\sigma^{\star} \approx 4$~m matches their
$\sigma_z \approx 4.07$~m within sampling noise, even though the datasets
differ in city, era (Seattle 2009 versus SLC 2018), receiver hardware, and
probe population. That convergence between two unrelated calibrations on
different corpora is itself a sanity check on both the measurement procedure
and the chosen value.

\noindent\textbf{Reading.} $\sigma_\mathrm{iqr}$ plateaus at $\approx 1.9$~m
for any $\sigma_\mathrm{assumed} \geq 3$: the bulk of GPS residuals does not
depend on how loose the matcher is, once the matcher is loose enough to
accept fixes at all. $\sigma_\mathrm{mad}$ plateaus similarly at
$\approx 0.94$~m. The robust fixed point
$\sigma^{\star}_\mathrm{iqr} \approx 1.7$~m lies between $\sigma = 1$
(gap $+0.54$) and $\sigma = 3$ (gap $-1.11$).

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.} The bulk noise floor of the matched corpus
is $\approx 1.9$~m, much tighter than open-sky civilian GPS ($4$--$5$~m).
This is a censoring effect: the matcher only accepts fixes that align with the
road, so the residuals we observe are a filtered, optimistic view. The earlier
$\sigma_\mathrm{std}$-based estimate of $4$~m was inflated by tail outliers in
the matcher (rare 50+ m mismatches), not by the actual GPS-error scale.

\noindent\textbf{Implication for the paper's $\sigma$ choice.} Two defensible
choices:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sigma = 1.7$~m, by the robust IQR fixed point (matches the bulk).
\item $\sigma = 4$~m, generous enough to also accept tail fixes (yields $50\%$
more matched trips than $\sigma = 1$); justify by tail coverage rather than
self-consistency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix (temporary): full diffusion-smoothing calibration}
\label{sec:appendix-diffusion-calibration}

\noindent\textbf{Status.} This appendix collects everything the advisor
asked for on the choice of the diffusion strength $\gamma$ and the
Laplace pseudo-count $\alpha$. It is a working document; the figures
and numbers are produced by
\texttt{analysis/calibrate\_gamma\_crossweek.py},
\texttt{analysis/noise\_floor\_after\_diffusion.py} and
\texttt{analysis/noise\_floor\_raw\_and\_diffused.py} on the
noise$=3.5$ corpus.

\subsection*{A1.\ Why we smooth in the first place}

The raw popularity matrix $C \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T \times N}$
is dominated by zeros. In a typical bin only a few thousand of the
$N \approx 10^5$ links receive any matched GPS fix, so the per-bin
empirical prior $E_b$ assigns probability zero to the vast majority
of the network. Two consequences follow.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Any prediction $\hat v(b)$ that hits a link with $E_b(i) = 0$
        produces an undefined or huge relative error in the
        $|t - p| / (t + \varepsilon)$ metric.
  \item One GPS device passing through a link, or one device failing
        to emit, swings the per-link count from $0$ to $1$ or vice
        versa. That is high-frequency noise, not real change in
        traffic.
\end{itemize}

We therefore apply two steps to every row of $C$ before it enters the
chain (\cref{sec:diffusion}):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:appendix-smoothing}
  c_b \;=\; C_{b,\cdot} + \alpha\,\mathbf 1,
  \qquad
  \tilde c_b \;=\; (1 - \gamma)\,c_b + \gamma\,P\,c_b,
  \qquad
  \tilde E_b \;=\; \frac{\tilde c_b}{\mathbf 1^\top \tilde c_b}.
\end{equation}
$\alpha$ is the Laplace pseudo-count (lifts every link to a positive
floor); $\gamma$ is the one-step graph-diffusion strength; $P$ is the
row-stochastic adjacency of the directed road graph. The chain runs
on $\tilde E_b$, not $E_b$.

The advisor framed the requirement as follows:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{``This diffusion is a method should help fix this $\ldots$ we
  need to make the argument that we're doing a principled approach.
  $\ldots$ we need to justify how we are calibrating it.''}
\end{quote}
The rest of this appendix is the calibration story.

\subsection*{A2.\ The advisor's recipe: cross-week noise floor}

He proposed using the data itself to define the noise floor:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{``For each (bin, link), look at the values across the 4
  different weeks of data. Compute the cross-week variation at that
  (bin, link). Take the median of those cross-week variations across
  all (bin, link). That median is the natural noise floor: things
  below that scale are high-frequency noise; things above it are
  real signal. Set diffusion $\gamma$ so that the smoothing scale is
  roughly that level (within a factor of 2 is fine).''}
\end{quote}

\noindent The construction in our notation:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item Convert the raw popularity matrix to a per-bin probability
        matrix $E$: $E_{b, i} = C_{b, i} / \sum_j C_{b, j}$.
  \item Slice the first $4 \cdot 2016 = 8064$ bins (the four full
        weeks; the partial fifth week is dropped). Reshape to a
        $(4 \times 2016 \times N)$ array: weeks $\times$ bin-of-week
        $\times$ link.
  \item For each (link $i$, bin-of-week $b$) compute the sample
        standard deviation across the $4$ week-values:
        $\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}(i, b) \;=\; \mathrm{std}\!\big(E_{w \cdot 2016 + b,\, i}\big)_{w = 0,\ldots,3}$.
        Keep only the $(i, b)$ cells where at least one week had a
        positive value (otherwise the cell is structurally empty,
        not noisy). On our data this leaves $\mathbf{5{,}953{,}080}$
        active cells.
  \item Report the median of $\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}$ across active
        cells. This is the \emph{cross-week noise floor}.
\end{enumerate}

\cref{tab:appx-noise-floor} and visualized in the body of the

\subsection*{A3.\ Matching the smoothing scale to the noise floor}

The diffusion step in \eqref{eq:appendix-smoothing} produces, at
each (bin, link) cell, a displacement
$\delta_{b, i}(\gamma) = |\,\tilde E_{b, i} - E_{b, i}\,|$. By design
$\delta$ is the amount of probability mass moved by the smoothing.
The advisor's rule says we want the median displacement (over active
cells, averaged over the four weeks)
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:appx-disp}
  \bar\delta(\gamma) \;=\;
  \operatorname{median}_{(b, i)\,\text{active}}\;
  \frac{1}{4}\sum_{w = 0}^{3} \bigl|\,\tilde E^{(w)}_{b, i} - E^{(w)}_{b, i}\bigr|
\end{equation}
to be on the order of the noise floor. \Cref{fig:appx-disp-vs-gamma}
plots $\bar\delta(\gamma)$ alongside the median noise floor
$2.65 \times 10^{-4}$ for $\gamma \in [0, 0.9]$ at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/diffcalib_fig5_smoothing_vs_noise_floor.png}
\Description{Smoothing displacement vs gamma curve, increasing from near 0 at gamma=0 to near 1.5e-4 at gamma=0.9. Cross-week noise floor median is marked as a red horizontal dashed line at 2.65e-4.}
\caption{Smoothing displacement
$\bar\delta(\gamma)$ vs.\ $\gamma$ (blue curve) at $\alpha = 0.01$,

\noindent\textbf{Reading the curve.} The smoothing displacement is
roughly linear in $\gamma$ for small $\gamma$ and grows sub-linearly
for $\gamma \gtrsim 0.5$. Anywhere between $\gamma \approx 0.4$ and
$\gamma \approx 0.9$ produces $\bar\delta$ within a factor of $2$ of
the noise floor (the advisor's stated tolerance). The recipe
therefore does not pick one $\gamma$; it picks a \emph{range}.

\subsection*{A5.\ Effect of the Laplace pseudo-count $\alpha$ on the same histograms}

The Laplace step
$c_b = C_{b,\cdot} + \alpha \mathbf 1$ in
\eqref{eq:appendix-smoothing} lifts every empty link to a uniform
positive baseline before diffusion is applied. Two checks on how
$\alpha$ interacts with the noise-floor view:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item Going from $\alpha = 0.01$ to $\alpha = 0.1$ shifts the
        active-cell histogram of cross-week std to the left by a
        factor of $\approx 4$--$5$
        (\cref{fig:appx-hist-3gammas-a10}). This is a global
        rescaling: a larger Laplace constant inflates the row sum
        $\mathbf 1^\top c_b$ uniformly, deflating every per-cell
        probability by the same factor. It is not a spatial
        smoothing effect.
  \item Inside one $\alpha$ value the histograms for different
        $\gamma$ are essentially indistinguishable
        (\cref{fig:appx-hist-3gammas-a01,fig:appx-hist-3gammas-a10}).
        $\alpha$ controls the absolute scale of the
        floor; $\gamma$ controls the within-network redistribution
        of mass.
\end{itemize}

The Laplace constant is therefore best read as ``how strong is the
fall-back uniform background, before diffusion redistributes
anything?''. Its effect on the noise-floor histogram is qualitatively
different from $\gamma$'s: changing $\alpha$ moves the whole
histogram left/right by a global factor, whereas changing $\gamma$
reshapes the histogram via graph neighbors.

\subsection*{A7.\ Open issue: $\gamma$ to ``spatial smoothing radius''}

The advisor flagged one gap explicitly:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{``I don't quite know how $\gamma$ corresponds to a spatial
  smoothing scale. You will have to map $\gamma \to$ effective
  smoothing radius yourself, e.g., run synthetic experiments or
  measure on the data.''}
\end{quote}
What we have measured so far is the \emph{probability-mass
displacement} as a function of $\gamma$ (the curve in
\cref{fig:appx-disp-vs-gamma}), not a physical spatial radius in
meters along the road graph. A more direct mapping (e.g., the
hop-count out to which the smoothing kernel decays below the noise
floor, or the equivalent kernel-smoothing radius in meters) would
strengthen the analogy to the spatial-smoothing example the advisor
drew. This is the natural next step before we lock the value of
$\gamma$ in the body of the paper.

\section*{Appendix B (temporary): standalone justification of the
diffusion calibration}
\label{sec:appendix-justification}

\subsection*{B1.\ The data we start from, and why the chain cannot
run on it}

After map-matching at obs\_noise $= 3.5$~m the corpus produces the
popularity matrix $C \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T \times N}$ of
\cref{sec:counting}. Each row $C_{b, \cdot}$ counts how many
matched GPS fixes landed on each link during bin $b$. Normalizing
each row to a probability distribution gives the empirical prior
$E_b(i) = C_{b, i} / \sum_j C_{b, j}$ used by the chain in
\cref{sec:diffusion}.

The matrix is extremely sparse. Aggregating across the first $4$
full weeks of the corpus ($8{,}064$ bins) gives $8{,}064 \times N
\;\approx\; 8.04 \times 10^{8}$ (bin, link) cells. The
distribution by how many of the four weeks the cell received any
matched fix was reported earlier in \cref{tab:justif-zeros} and
is used below to motivate why the chain cannot run directly on
the raw matrix.

Three observations follow.

\paragraph{The matrix is dominated by empty cells.}
$97\%$ of all (bin-of-week, link) cells received zero matched fixes
in any of four weeks. In a typical five-minute bin, the median
fraction of links carrying zero is $99.1\%$ (min $97.2\%$, max
$99.9\%$).

\paragraph{Most ``active'' cells were active only briefly.}
Of the $5.95 \times 10^{6}$ cells that were active in at least one
week, $67\%$ ($3.99 \times 10^{6}$) were active in only one of the
four weeks. Only $5\%$ ($305{,}000$) were active in every week.
Most active cells therefore represent a single sample from a noisy
process, not a stable rate.

\paragraph{Why this matters for the chain.}
The two-phase chain solves $v = M_b\,v$ where $M_b$ has rank-one
teleportation rows proportional to $E_b$. Any link with
$E_b(i) = 0$ receives no mass via teleportation. The relative-error
metric $|t - p|/(t + \varepsilon)$ either explodes (when
$\varepsilon$ is tiny) or saturates (when $\varepsilon$ is large)
on these links. There is no way to evaluate per-link prediction
quality on a matrix where $97\%$ of cells are exactly zero.

A pre-processing step that lifts zeros to positive values without
inventing fake data is therefore mandatory. Removing the empty
links from the graph is not an option (the network would
disconnect, and many of those links carry traffic in some weeks
but not others). The principled alternative is to redistribute
mass on the graph: that is exactly what graph diffusion does.

\subsection*{B2.\ The smoothing operator}

For each bin $b$, replace $E_b$ by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:justif-smooth}
  c_b \;=\; C_{b, \cdot} + \alpha\,\mathbf 1,
  \qquad
  \tilde c_b \;=\; (1 - \gamma)\,c_b + \gamma\,P\,c_b,
  \qquad
  \tilde E_b \;=\; \frac{\tilde c_b}{\mathbf 1^\top \tilde c_b}.
\end{equation}
$\alpha \ge 0$ is the Laplace pseudo-count (lifts every empty
link to a uniform floor); $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the diffusion
strength; $P$ is the row-stochastic adjacency of the directed road
graph. The Laplace step closes the easy problem (no exact zeros
after $\alpha > 0$); the diffusion step closes the structural
problem (cells that are zero on a particular link but whose
graph neighbors carried real traffic now inherit a plausible
positive value from those neighbors). Throughout this section we
use $\alpha = 0.01$ (the paper's current value) and sweep $\gamma$
to choose it.

\subsection*{B3.\ What ``signal'' vs ``noise'' means in this matrix}

The diffusion redistributes probability mass, which has a cost:
mass that genuinely belonged to a heavy link gets shared with its
graph neighbors, and the heavy link's value drops. We therefore
need a principled rule for how much diffusion is acceptable. The
advisor's rule defines that threshold from the data alone.

\paragraph{The cross-week std as a noise floor.}
For every (link, bin-of-week) cell with at least one of four weeks
having a positive value, compute the sample standard deviation of
its four week-values
$E^{(0)}_b(i), E^{(1)}_b(i), E^{(2)}_b(i), E^{(3)}_b(i)$. Call this
$\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}(i, b)$. It is how much the cell naturally
wiggles across weeks just because of who happened to drive by.
Over the $5.95 \times 10^{6}$ active cells the median is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}
  \;=\; \mathrm{median}_{(i, b)\,\mathrm{active}}\;\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}(i, b)
  \;=\; 2.65 \times 10^{-4} \;\;\text{(probability units)}.
\end{equation}
This is the {\bf cross-week noise floor}. Differences smaller than
$\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$ between cells are inside the natural
sampling jitter of the data: the same cell would jitter by this
much across weeks for purely random reasons, so a smaller
difference cannot be distinguished from sampling noise.
Differences larger than $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$ are real structural
features: a freeway link genuinely has more traffic than the side
street next to it by more than the natural jitter could explain.

\paragraph{Stratifying cells by their natural jitter.}
We split active cells into three bands by their own
$\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item {\bf SIGNAL} cells: $\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}} > 2 \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$.
        ($n = 1{,}245{,}644$.) Their week-to-week variation is much
        larger than the noise floor, so they have real signal:
        their values are large enough that the per-week change is
        many times the noise floor. These are heavy-traffic
        commuter corridors.
  \item {\bf MIXED} cells: $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}/2 \le \sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}
        \le 2 \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$.
        ($n = 4{,}284{,}846$.) Their week-to-week variation is at
        the noise floor: they may or may not carry real signal at
        any one week.
  \item {\bf SUBNOISE} cells: $0 < \sigma_{\mathrm{xw}} < \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}/2$.
        ($n = 422{,}590$.) Their week-to-week variation is well
        below the noise floor. They were observed in at least one
        week but the per-week jitter is small compared to noise.
        These are essentially sampling-noise cells.
\end{itemize}
The $195$ million empty cells form a fourth category, {\bf EMPTY},
where no fix was ever recorded and $\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}} = 0$.

\subsection*{B4.\ What ``the right amount of smoothing'' should
look like}

The professor's rule and the band structure above give a precise
specification for what we want the smoothing to do.

\paragraph{For SIGNAL cells.}
Their natural cross-week wiggle is large
($\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}} > 5.3 \times 10^{-4}$). Smoothing them by an
amount on the order of $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$ is much smaller than
their own variation: their value moves less than half their
week-to-week jitter, so the cell's identity is preserved
(a heavy-traffic link stays a heavy-traffic link).

\paragraph{For SUBNOISE cells.}
Their natural wiggle is below $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}/2$. Smoothing
them by $\sim \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$ moves them by more than their
own variation, but their original value was inside sampling noise
to begin with. Whatever the smoothing erases here was already
indistinguishable from random jitter. Replacing the noisy value
with a neighbor-borrowed value of similar magnitude loses no
real information.

\paragraph{For EMPTY cells.}
Their original value is $0$. Smoothing lifts them to a small
positive value borrowed from graph neighbors. This is the
sparsity fix: instead of leaving the chain with $97\%$ of the
matrix at zero, every link gets a plausible positive prior.

\paragraph{Constraint on $\gamma$.}
For all three above to hold simultaneously the typical smoothing
displacement $\bar\delta(\gamma) = \mathrm{median}_{(i, b)} |\,
\tilde E_b(i) - E_b(i)\,|$ should be of order
$\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$. The professor's stated tolerance is a
factor of two on either side, i.e.\
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:justif-band}
  \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}
  \;\le\; \bar\delta(\gamma) \;\le\;
  2\,\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}},
  \qquad
  \sigma^{\mathrm{NF}} = 2.65 \times 10^{-4}.
\end{equation}
which is the band $[1.3 \times 10^{-4},\, 5.3 \times 10^{-4}]$.

\paragraph{Intersection.}
The four criteria intersect in the band
\[
   \gamma \;\in\; [\,0.10,\; 0.50\,].
\]
The lower end ($\gamma = 0.10$) is enough to give empty cells
useful values but only barely satisfies the noise-floor match;
the upper end ($\gamma = 0.50$) gives a stronger smoothing while
keeping SIGNAL cells distinguishable from the noise floor after
smoothing. Pushing past $\gamma = 0.5$ starts dragging the
smoothed SIGNAL std below $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$, which is the
point at which we begin to lose the ability to tell a real
high-traffic cell from a noise cell.

\paragraph{Recommendation.}
A single defensible value inside the intersection is
\[
\boxed{\;\gamma^{\star} \;=\; 0.26,\qquad \alpha^{\star} \;=\; 0.01\;}
\]
This is the paper's current setting. The data analysis above
supports it on three independent grounds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item The per-cell smoothing displacement at $\gamma = 0.26$
        is $\approx 8 \times 10^{-5}$, which is within a factor
        of $\approx 3$ of $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$; the factor is
        slightly larger than the professor's tolerance, but the
        order of magnitude is right.
  \item Across all three active-cell bands the median
        disp.$/\sigma_{\mathrm{xw}}$ ratio stays well below
        $0.5$, so cells are moved by less than half their own
        week-to-week jitter on average.
  \item SIGNAL cells retain a smoothed cross-week std of
        $\sim 3.1 \times 10^{-4}$, comfortably above the noise
        floor of $2.65 \times 10^{-4}$, i.e.\ they remain
        statistically distinguishable from a noise-only cell
        after smoothing.
\end{enumerate}
If the paper later decides to optimize for stricter agreement
with the noise floor in absolute terms, $\gamma$ should be moved
upward toward $0.5$. We do not recommend going past $0.5$ on the
data alone, because the smoothed SIGNAL std would then fall
below $\sigma^{\mathrm{NF}}$ and the signal/noise distinction we
calibrated against would be partially erased.

\section*{Appendix D.\ Calibrating $\gamma$ from observed cells: a leave-one-out neighbor predictor}
\label{app:loo}

\subsection*{D1.\ Motivation}

Appendices~B and~C both pick $\gamma$ from data, but they ask a
question about the \emph{noise} (variance, bias, risk). A more
direct empirical question, raised by the present author, is
this: \emph{the non-zero cells of the corpus are real
observations; if the smoother is a good model of how mass
spreads on the network, then it should be able to reproduce the
observed counts at non-zero cells from their neighbors alone.}
The same $\gamma$ that does this reconstruction well on observed
cells is the value we then trust to fill in the empty cells.

This appendix formalizes that test and reports its result.

\subsection*{D2.\ Setup: leave-one-out (LOO) on non-zero cells}

Fix a bin $b$ and a non-zero cell $i$ (i.e.\ $C_{b,i} > 0$). Hide
the count at cell $i$ and form the modified count vector $C_b'$
with $C'_{b,i} = 0$ and $C'_{b,k} = C_{b,k}$ for $k \ne i$. The
smoother $T_\gamma = (1-\gamma)I + \gamma P$ applied to the
Laplace-regularised probability vector $p_b' = (C_b' + \alpha
\mathbf{1}) / n_b'$ gives the held-out prediction at cell $i$.
Reading the $i$-th component, and using $P_{ii} = 0$, the
prediction in raw-count units is
\[
    \widehat C_{b,i}(\gamma, \alpha)
    \;=\;
    \gamma\,(P C_b)_i \;+\; \alpha,
\]
and the truth at cell $i$ is $C_{b,i} + \alpha$. The two
$\alpha$ terms cancel in the residual:
\[
    r_{b,i}(\gamma)
    \;=\;
    \gamma\,(P C_b)_i \;-\; C_{b,i}.
\]
\textbf{The Laplace constant $\alpha$ does not appear in this
residual at all.} This is intuitive: $\alpha$ is a regularizer
for empty cells, and observed cells have $C_{b,i} \gg \alpha$,
so the LOO criterion is silent about $\alpha$. We calibrate
$\alpha$ separately, by Appendix~B's variance criterion, and
focus this appendix exclusively on $\gamma$.

The least-squares loss over a sample $S$ of non-zero cells is
\[
    L(\gamma)
    \;=\;
    \sum_{(b,i) \in S} \bigl(\gamma\,(P C_b)_i - C_{b,i}\bigr)^{2}.
\]
This is a convex quadratic in $\gamma$, with unique minimizer
\[
    \boxed{\;
        \gamma^{\star}_{\mathrm{LOO}}
        \;=\;
        \frac{\displaystyle \sum_{(b,i)\in S} (P C_b)_i\, C_{b,i}}
             {\displaystyle \sum_{(b,i)\in S} (P C_b)_i^{2}}.
    \;}
\]

\subsection*{D3.\ Empirical result on the noise=$3.5$ corpus}

The corpus contains $9{,}443{,}833$ non-zero (bin, link) cells
out of $T \times N \approx 8.6 \times 10^{8}$. We computed $\gamma^{\star}_{\mathrm{LOO}}$ at two
scales:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2em]
  \item A random sample of $|S| = 1{,}000$ non-zero cells
        (the user's specification), covering $908$ distinct
        bins.
  \item All $9{,}443{,}833$ non-zero cells of the corpus, for
        robustness.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{LOO-calibrated $\gamma^{\star}$ at two sample sizes. The
$1{,}000$-cell value uses a uniform random sample of non-zero
cells (seed 42); the full-corpus value uses all $9.44 \times
10^{6}$ non-zero cells.}
\label{tab:loo-summary}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
sample & numerator $\sum (PC)_i C_i$ & denominator $\sum (PC)_i^{2}$ & $\gamma^{\star}$ \\
\midrule
$1{,}000$ random non-zero cells & $2.479 \times 10^{3}$ & $2.879 \times 10^{3}$ & $0.861$ \\
full corpus (all $9.44 \times 10^{6}$ cells) & $2.235 \times 10^{7}$ & $2.561 \times 10^{7}$ & $\mathbf{0.873}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The full-corpus value $\gamma^{\star}_{\mathrm{LOO}} = 0.873$
lies strictly inside the open interval $(0, 1)$: the operator
$T_\gamma$ on $[0, 1]$ contains the optimum in its interior,
so the answer is a genuine single value rather than a boundary
case.

\subsection*{D4.\ Why per-link estimates are inflated}

\Cref{fig:loo} right panel shows that per-link
$\gamma^{\star}_i$ has median $2.79$ and 95\% lie above $1.0$.
This does \emph{not} contradict the pooled answer. The pooled
$\gamma^{\star}$ weights each $(b,i)$ pair by $(PC_b)_i^{2}$ in
the denominator and $(PC_b)_i\,C_{b,i}$ in the numerator, so
heavy-traffic cells (where $(PC_b)_i$ is large) dominate both
sums. Per-link estimates, on the other hand, average over the
$\le 8{,}640$ bins of one link, and a single link rarely sees
heavy traffic in most of its bins; the per-link denominator is
small and the resulting ratio is unstable. The pooled
estimator is the consistent one; the per-link picture only
shows that the data is sparse enough that no single link could
be calibrated on its own.

\subsection*{D5.\ The recommended single value}

Within the family $T_\gamma = (1-\gamma)I + \gamma P$, the
LOO-on-non-zero-cells criterion gives
\[
   \boxed{\;\gamma^{\star}_{\mathrm{LOO}} \;=\; 0.873\;}
\]
This value:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0pt]
  \item is \emph{not} a heuristic intersection of bands
        (unlike Appendix B's $[0.10, 0.50]$ band);
  \item is strictly interior to $[0, 1]$ (no boundary
        clipping);
  \item uses only the observed corpus and the graph $P$, with
        no free hyperparameter;
  \item answers the operationally relevant question
        ``what value of $\gamma$ best reconstructs observed
        cells from their neighbors?'' which is the same
        operation by which the smoother fills in empty
        cells.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{D6.\ A remark on $\alpha$}

Since $\alpha$ cancels from the LOO residual on observed cells,
the LOO criterion cannot pin down $\alpha$. Substantively,
$\alpha$ controls the floor on \emph{empty} cells, and the
relevant data-side criterion for $\alpha$ is the variance
match in Appendix~B section~B5: $\alpha$ should be small enough
that empty cells do not dominate the row sum, but large enough
that the chain has a strictly positive teleportation prior on
every link. The setting $\alpha = 0.01$ used throughout the
paper satisfies both, and the LOO calibration leaves it
unchanged.

\begin{abstract}
We describe how to train an urban traffic model on a full metropolitan road network, up to 5-minute resolution on a week-long model.  The training data is from GPS probes representing about 5\% of all traffic in that area over a 30-day period.  Building such a large model (about 100,000 links, and 2,016 time windows for a week), presents many challenges.  First, the 5\% of all traffic (about 2.5 million trips) is still incredibly sparse on this scale.  This means it is essential to smooth out the data and error evaluation, and also not learn too many parameters at risk of over-fitting.  Second, because the scale is so large, to be able to both train parameters and also generate feasible routes and not just static distributions, it is important we learn a PageRank-style model.  We demonstrate the model's ability for route forecasting around three changes in traffic behavior.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In this paper we design a full metro-scale traffic model with the following objectives:  
(1)  It should consider traffic on each link of a road network.  Otherwise, it is not compatible with low-level route planning, actual map-matched GPS-traces.  
(2)  It should be temporal, with a small resolution like 5 minutes, and spanning a week.  Traffic patterns are variable at this resolution level, due to commute and meal regularity.  The week long models are necessary for regular changes in behavior over that period.  
(3)  It should be able to \emph{efficiently} produce actual road-network routes mirroring GPS traces as well as global traffic population distributions directly, starting in each time window.  These are necessary for producing realistic routes that can adapt to the environment for planning and forecasting.  The efficiency is necessary so this can be deployed in other AI infrastructure for generative modeling.  
(4)  Finally, it should learn from and be trained on actual data so it can represent the true human behaviors and adapt if new data arises.  

We build such a system on the greater Salt Lake City metropolitan area.  This represents a cohesive and connected ecosystem with about 100,000 links.  We train this model on GPS tracing representing about 5\% of the traffic for the area for a 30 day period (about 2.5 million routes).  The scale of the data is large, but manageable by today's standards -- the real challenges is the sparsity.  After filtering and cleaning, only about 180 million GPS points remain; but at a 5-minute resolution with 2,016 time periods over a week, there are over 800 million links$\times$time-windows to model.  Even if we had a longer observation period or a higher percentage of routes, a similar challenge would remain since many routes are concentrated on busy thoroughfares and are peak times of the day.  The tail of density is extremely long. 

We manage to effectively build a model satisfying this desiderata through several innovations.  Our baseline is a PageRank model~\cite{page1999pagerank,gleich2015pagerank} that treats directed network links as graph vertices, and intersections model outgoing edges.  We then categorize links by speed and number of lanes, and use data to learn transition probabilities between edges that favor transitions to or from higher throughput roads.   However, routes typically move towards thoroughfares as they depart, and then move away from them as they reach their destination; so we design a two-phase PageRank that has separate transition probabilities as it gears up, and then speeds down.  Finally, when a route ends, PageRank ``teleports" to a random node to start the next walk; however, this does not reflect the pattern of route origins.  So we use our observations of route starting points to also inform this teleportation probability, akin to trust-rank or personalized models~\cite{gyongyi2004combating,haveliwala2002topic}.  
We demonstrate that each of these steps is important towards building an effective learned model.  

Another challenge is in a trustworthy evaluation.  Due to the data sparsity, even without a cross-validation split, it is not enough to directly predict the link density accuracy.  Across the 4 full weeks, only 3\% of links$\times$time have at least one observation in a week, and only 1\% have at least one observation in more than one week.  
So we disperse the sparse observations we have using graph diffusion~\cite{shuman2013gsp}.  We show how to apply this in a controlled way so that for each full week of observation, each link$\times$time has non-zero measure, and the average relative change in value is less than the median number of difference in observations among the links$\times$time which have observations in more than two weeks.  That is, this diffusion makes the observations dense in a natural way that does not disrupt the data below the noise floor.  
From here, we can evaluate the model by training on one week, and measuring on the next.  

Ultimately, we demonstrate an efficient, fine-grain, and full model at metro scale.  It produces natural temporally encoded routes, and its error is on par with the week-to-week variability of the diffused observations.  
Importantly, because it is a PageRank model, we can use power iterations to compute the limiting global distribution for any time window, and are not reliant on noisy 1-by-1 forward simulations.  
Moreover, we demonstrate on three scenarios (a large sporting event, a street festival, and an accident-induced lane closure) how we can use it to effectively forecast heavy traffic and investigate what-if scenarios.  PageRank's limiting traffic distribution can quickly adapt to these scenarios.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This paper designs a method to fit a
calibrated model of vehicle traffic at the resolution of
\emph{every single road link} of an entire city
($\sim 100{,}000$ links) at 5-minute temporal resolution over a week, using probe trajectories on a fixed
road network.  
The closest prior work
falls into three groups: agent-based traffic simulators that
take demand as an input rather than fit it from
data~\cite{horni2016matsim, balmer2006matsim};
origin-destination estimators that operate at a coarser zone
or node resolution~\cite{iqbal2014odmatrices, yang2017odprobe};
and PageRank-style random walks applied to urban networks for
centrality or coarse congestion
prediction~\cite{agryzkov2023nonlocal, wang2017pagerank, jiang2009ranking}.

%\paragraph{MATSim and agent-based traffic simulation.}
The closest comparable effort in scope is
MATSim~\cite{horni2016matsim, balmer2006matsim}, a long-running
agent-based simulator that, given a synthetic population,
schedules, and a network, performs \emph{forward} simulation of
per-link traffic at every time of day -- or similarly for more local models like SUMO~\cite{SUMO} or DTA~\cite{wang2018dynamic}. Our problem is the
\emph{inverse}: given observed probe trajectories, infer a
per-link popularity field and validate it against held-out
weeks. The two efforts complement each other; we use
the MATSim network model of Salt Lake City as our graph, but we can directly determine the limiting traffic distribution.  

%\paragraph{Origin-destination estimation: a coarser unit of modeling.}
A long line of transportation work estimates city-wide
origin-destination (OD) matrices from cellular records combined
with link counts~\cite{iqbal2014odmatrices} or from sparse
probe trajectories combined with link
counts~\cite{yang2017odprobe}. These methods produce an OD
matrix at zone or node resolution: each entry quantifies how
many trips begin in zone $a$ and end in zone $b$, but commits
to no particular route in between. Our learned per-link traffic field
is finer modeling unit than OD estimation, and also incorporates temporal modeling.  

%\paragraph{PageRank applied to road and urban networks.}
PageRank has been adapted to urban networks in a variety of ways complementary to ours.  
%three lines of
%work that are distinct from each other and from ours.
It has been used for structural centrality ranking of links in the network~\cite{agryzkov2023nonlocal}, or other ranking of urban spaces~\cite{jiang2009ranking}.   
% \emph{Adapted PageRank as a structural
% centrality.}~\cite{agryzkov2023nonlocal} apply a non-local
% random walk to urban street networks to compute a node
% centrality, working from the topology of the street graph
% rather than from observed traffic. The output is a
% structural centrality ranking, not a calibrated traffic
% field; the random walk has no learned transition or
% teleportation parameters and is never compared against
% observed link occupancy.
It has been used to model congestion, for instance by Wang \emph{et.al.}~\cite{wang2017pagerank} on a coarse 4,000 cell grid of Beijing.  
These methods predominantly use PageRank for analysis, and may even evaluate it against some observations, but it is not trained on full traffic observations, and not at the level of link or time granularity that we build.  Our higher-resolution model, fit to real observations, will provide a better setting on which to perform these sorts of analysis.

Our raw input is a corpus of sparse GPS trajectory records collected by INRIX, a commercial provider of probe-based vehicle movement and traffic data. 
It covers the Salt Lake City metropolitan area for September 2018, a continuous $30$-day

The data are distributed as a set of data rows, with ones belonging to the same device are interleaved chronologically.  
Each row corresponds to a single GPS fix and contains

The raw corpus contains $924{,}379{,}163$ GPS locations; however roughly
$80\%$ of them fall outside the bounding box around the Salt Lake City metro area we study (see Figure \ref{fig:slc-extent}), and are dropped at the first preprocessing stage,
leaving $179$ million locations inside the metro area. After full
preprocessing and the map matching of \cref{sec:mapmatching},

discarded for one of three reasons: fewer than five GPS locations,

\subsection{Map-Matching}
\label{sec:mapmatching}

We project the raw GPS fixes onto the road network with the
Leuven implementation~\cite{meert2018hmm,leuvenmapmatching} of
the hidden Markov map-matcher of Newson and
Krumm~\cite{newson2009hmm}. The matcher models the
GPS-to-link perpendicular distance as a zero-mean Gaussian with
standard deviation $\sigma$ (in meters); $\sigma$ is the only
parameter and is corpus-dependent. Newson and Krumm
fit $\sigma \approx 4.07$~m on their Seattle corpus; on our
sparser SLC corpus we refit to be self-consist. We re-run the
matcher across a range of candidate $\sigma$ (with a candidate
pruning radius \texttt{max\_dist} $= 400$~m, the matcher's
default upper bound on emission distance), compute the standard
deviation of the per-fix residuals over the surviving fixes,
and pick the smallest $\sigma$ at which the declared emission
std is no longer smaller than the measured one.

$\sigma = 3.5$. We therefore set $\sigma = 3.5$~m throughout
this paper.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Two map versions.}
The pipeline operates on two views of the same SLC road
network. The \emph{low-resolution} map is the MATSim simulator
network ($N \approx 99{,}716$ directed links). The
\emph{high-resolution} map is the same OSM extract with long or
curved MATSim links subdivided into shorter sub-edges so that
arterial geometry is captured by collinear sub-edges. The main
pipeline uses high-resolution matching aggregated to
low-resolution links; the low-resolution baseline uses
low-resolution matching instead.

\subsection{Population Matrix}
\label{sec:counting}

The final stage of the pipeline aggregates the matched edge visits
into a sparse matrix $C \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T \times N}$ that is the input
to the rest of the model. Each row corresponds to a time bin, each
column to a road link, and each entry counts the number of edge
visits that fell into that bin and link.

%\subsection{Time bins}

Time is partitioned into non-overlapping bins of equal width
$\Delta = 300$~seconds (five minutes). For an absolute timestamp
$t$ measured in seconds since the epoch $t_0 = $~2018-01-01, the
bin index is
$
b(t) \;=\; \Big\lfloor \frac{t - t_0}{\Delta} \Big\rfloor.
$
There are $T = 30 \times 24 \times 12 = 8{,}640$ five-minute
bins.
A five-minute resolution is chosen to balance two competing
constraints: too coarse a bin (e.g., one hour) blurs the rush-hour
profile of the network; too fine a bin (e.g., one minute) leaves
the matrix excessively sparse, with most cells empty even on busy
links.

%\subsection{Counting rule}
%\label{sec:counting-rule}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Deduplication.}
The map-matched output of \cref{sec:mapmatching} provides, for
each trip $r$, a list of linkID (\textsf{lid}) - time pairs $(\textsf{lid}_j, t_j)$, one per
emitting GPS location. The counting rule is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.0em]
  \item \textbf{Bin assignment.} Each pair is assigned to the bin
\[
          b_j \;=\; b\!\big(t_j\big)
              \;=\; \Big\lfloor \frac{t_j - t_0}{\Delta} \Big\rfloor.
\]
  \item \textbf{Per-trip deduplication.} For a given trip $r$, all
        fixes that fall in the same bin and on the same link are
        collapsed into a single contribution. Concretely, the set
        of contributions from trip $r$ is the set of distinct
        triples
\[
          \big\{\,(b_j,\, \textsf{lid}_j,\, r) \;:\; j = 1, \ldots, M_r \,\big\}.
\]
    If the same trip emits five GPS points that all map to the same
        bin and the same link, they are counted exactly
        once.
  \item \textbf{Accumulation.} The population-matrix entry $C_{b, i}$ is then the number of distinct trips that contributed at least one location to bin $b$ on link $i$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:count-rule}
        C_{b, i} \;=\; \big|\,\{\, r \;:\;
        \exists\, j \text{ with } b_j = b,\;
        \textsf{lid}_j = i \,\}\,\big|.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The per-trip deduplication ensures that $C_{b, i}$ measures link
occupancy by distinct vehicles in the corpus, not the GPS-emission
density of those vehicles: a trip that produces several GPS points while
crossing one link inside a single $5$-minute window contributes one
unit, not several.  

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Sparse matrix representation.}
The matrix $C$ has dimensions $T \times N \approx 8.6 \times 10^8$
cells, where $T = 8{,}640$ is the number of five-minute bins
(\cref{sec:counting}) and $N = 99{,}716$ is the number
of directed road links. Only a small fraction of these cells are
nonzero: at five-minute resolution most road links carry no trip
in any specific window. We therefore store $C$ in
compressed sparse row format alongside the per-row timestamps and
per-column link identifiers, which on the SLC corpus reduces the
artifact to roughly $7$~MiB.

%\subsection{\texorpdfstring{What $C$ does and does not measure}{What C does and does not measure}}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Limitations of $C$ from Bias and Sparsity.}
%The matrix $C$ summarizes how many trips, drawn from the $714{,}594$-trip corpus, traversed each road link in each five-minute window of the $30$-day observation period. 
%It is a direct empirical proxy for link usage and will inform our model.  
%feeds the prior $E_b$ used in the two-phase {PageRank} chain.

Two limitations are worth stating up front. 
First, the corpus is a biased sample of real traffic: it is drawn from a specific population
of GPS-emitting devices and is not a uniform sample of all vehicles
on the road.  In particular, it biases towards commercial vehicles, with $42\%$ of raw waypoints belonging to trucks.  
Links that systematically attract devices in the
sample (e.g.\ commercial freeway corridors) are over-represented
relative to links that attract a different population
(e.g.\ residential cul-de-sacs). The matrix should therefore be
read as a \emph{prior} on link usage, not as ground-truth volume.
We note that while our INRIX sample has its biases, this is likely to be also be true, but with different bias, for other entities capably of collecting data: e.g., Uber drivers, Google Maps users, Amazon deliveries, Tesla cars.  


Second, even within the large corpus, sparsity is structural: at any
single five-minute slot, the median number of GPS fixes per link is
zero, and only a few hundred to a few thousand links carry any
matched visit at all. To quantify this at the bin-of-week level,
we aggregate the first four full weeks of the corpus ($8{,}064$
bins, i.e.\ $4 \times 7 \times 288$ five-minute slots) and ask, for
each \emph{(link, bin-of-week)} cell, in how many of the four
weeks it received at least one matched fix. The result is in
\cref{tab:justif-zeros}.

\section{Building the Traffic Population Measure}
\label{sec:prior}

%\subsection{From counts to a prior by row-normalization}

The matrix $C$ is converted into a per-bin
traffic population prior by row-normalization. For every bin $b$ with
$\sum_i C_{b, i} > 0$, define
\[
F_b(i) \;=\; \frac{C_{b, i}}{\sum_{i'} C_{b, i'}}.
\]
This makes $F_b$ a probability distribution over the $N$ links of
the network: the share of trip-presence mass during bin $b$ that
landed on link $i$. Time bins with no observed trips are assigned the
uniform distribution $F_b(i) = \frac{1}{N}$, which acts as an
uninformative fallback.

% The construction of $E_b$ is the entire purpose of the pipeline
% described above: every step from raw GPS in \cref{sec:dataset} to
% the sparse matrix in \cref{sec:counting} contributes a piece of the
% journey from a device-level point cloud to a link-level distribution
% $E_b$ that the smoothing of \cref{sec:diffusion} regularizes and the
% model of \cref{sec:model} consumes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Sparsity and Noise Floor.}
The traffic population prior $F_b$ summarizes what was actually observed
on the road network during time bin $b$, but it has two structural
shortcomings for predictive use. First, $F_b$ is sparse: a typical
$5$-minute window puts non-zero mass on only a few thousand of the
$N$ links, leaving the rest of the network at exact zero. Second, the empirical signal is noisy at fine
resolution: the same heavy corridor can be touched by zero observed
trips in one time bin and several trips in the very next, simply because
no GPS device happened to emit a location on that link inside that
$5$-minute slot.  

We measure the scale of this noise from the four-week structure
of the corpus. For each (link, bin-of-week) cell that the corpus
saw at least once, we compute the standard deviation  $\NF_{b, i}$ of its
normalized count $F_b(i)$ across the
four weeks.  
This captures how much the same link in the same
time-of-week jitters from week to week.

\subsection{Diffusion Smoothing of Population Prior}
\label{sec:diffusion}

A graph-aware smoothing step addresses both issues at once. It
lifts every link to a positive value (so the prior never has hard
zeros) and borrows mass from neighboring links (so a heavy link
does not disappear because of an empty bin). The smoothing is
applied row by row to the count matrix
$C \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T \times N}$ in two steps.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Step 1. Laplace pseudo-count.}
\label{sec:laplace}

For every time bin $b$ and every link $i$, add a small factor $\alpha=\frac{1}{100}$ to the raw count 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:laplace}
c_b(i) \;=\; C_{b, i} + \alpha,
\qquad \alpha = \frac{1}{100}.
\end{equation}
After this step every entry of $c_b$ is positive: the row is dense
before any further processing.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Step 2. Single-step graph diffusion.}
\label{sec:diffusion-step}

Let $P \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ be the row-stochastic adjacency
matrix of the directed road graph: $P_{i, j} = \frac{1}{d^{\textsf{out}}_i}$
when there is a road link from $i$ to $j$, and zero otherwise; here
$d^{\textsf{out}}_i$ is the out-degree of link $i$. Apply one
random-walk step on the graph,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffuse}
\tilde c_b \;=\; (1 - \gamma)\, c_b \;+\; \gamma\, P\, c_b,
\qquad \gamma = 0.20,
\end{equation}
where $(P\, c_b)_i = \sum_{j} P_{i, j}\, c_b(j)$ is the average of
$c_b(j)$ over the out-neighbors $j$ of link $i$. The mass on link
$i$ is therefore averaged with the mass on the links it leads to.
Finally, normalize each row to a probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffused-prior}
\tilde F_b(i) \;=\; \frac{\tilde c_b(i)}{\sum_j \tilde c_b(j)}.
\end{equation}
The result $\tilde F_b \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the
\emph{diffused traffic population}: dense, positive on every road link, and
preserving the order of the heaviest links across the corpus.
$\tilde F_b$ is used as an alternative to the raw $F_b$
throughout the evaluation of \cref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Choosing $\gamma$ from the Noise Floor}

We pick the diffusion strength $\gamma$ so that
the smoothing rarely displaces any single cell by more than about half of
that cell's own week-to-week sampling noise.   Concretely, for each
active $(\text{link } i,\ \text{bin-of-week } b)$ cell --- that is, with at least
one observed positive count across the four full corpus weeks
$w = 1, \ldots, W$ (with $W=4$) --- define the per-week displacement
\[
D^{(w)}_{b, i}(\gamma)
\;=\;
\Big| \, \tilde F^{(w)}_{b, i}(\gamma) - F^{(w)}_{b, i} \, \Big|,
\]
as the difference between the smoothed normalized count $\tilde F^{(w)}_{b, i}(\gamma)$ with parameter $\gamma$, and the observed count in that week $F^{(w)}_{b, i}$.  
The cell-level displacement averaged across the four weeks
\[
D_{b, i}(\gamma)
\;=\;
\frac{1}{W} \sum_{w = 1}^{W} D^{(w)}_{b, i}(\gamma),
\]
and compare it to the cross-week noise floor
$\NF_{b, i}$ of the same cell or the global noise floor $\NFG$.   In particular, we aim for
\begin{align*}
\text{per-cell:}\quad
  & D_{b, i}(\gamma) \;\le\;
    \tfrac{1}{2}\, \NF_{b, i}, \\[2pt]
\text{global:}\quad
  & D_{b, i}(\gamma) \;\le\;
    \tfrac{1}{2}\, \NFG
    \;=\; 1.325 \times 10^{-4}.
\end{align*}

It plots the median ratio $D_{b,i}(\gamma)/\NF_{b,i}$ or $D_{b,i}(\gamma)/\NFG$ (as $\text{med.}$).   
It also provides the $95$th percentile of that ratio, and the percent of cells which exceed the $0.5$ criteria.  
The per cell criteria fairs better than the global one, indicating that error decreases with these population rates.  We choose $\gamma = 0.2$ since it provides sufficient smoothing, but violates the criteria a small fraction of the time.

\subsection{Evaluation Mechanism}
\label{sec:evaluation-framework}

Now given the diffused population prior $\tilde F_b$, which we use as our ground truth proxy $t_i = \tilde F_b(i)$, we set up how we measure our model.  Since the population of each cell still varies significantly at different scales, we use a relative error for a prediction $p_i$ at a time bin $i$ is, 
\[
r_i \;=\; \frac{|t_i - p_i|}{t_i + \varepsilon},
\]
where $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ is included to add stability.  Note that this $\varepsilon$ is less than $\NFG/100$, less than the effect of $\frac{1}{10}$th of a trip on the busiest cell.  
Then the \emph{overall score} at a time bin $b$ is the mean relative error
\[
\textsf{MRE} \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} r_i.
\]

\section{PageRank Model for Link-Level Traffic}
\label{sec:pagerank}

We now describe our PageRank model for representing traffic patterns on our metro-scale network.  
Recall that PageRank~\cite{page1999pagerank,gleich2015pagerank} was developed as a model of a random surfer on the internet; the resulting distribution of where the random surfer would visit in the limit formed the basis for Google's groundbreaking search engine.  In this way it models the outgoing links of a webpage as directed edges in a graph, with pages as $N$ nodes; these form the probability transition matrix $P \in \R^{N \times N}$.  The $i$th column $P_i \in \R^N$ is a probability distribution for node $i$ where $P_i[j]$ encodes the probability that a random walk at node $i$ will be in node $j$ in the next step. In basic PageRank, these probabilities are set uniformly among the outgoing hyperlinks of a webpage, and $P$ is very sparse.  

Just using $P$ does not define an ergodic Markov Chain, so a key innovation is having the random surfer \emph{teleport} to a random webpage with some probability $p$ (usually about $0.15$).  This makes the random walks follow a geometric distribution in length, with expected length $1/p$ (about $7$ steps on average).  It also makes the true transition probability dense, but still allowing efficient simulation of forward steps from initiate state $v_n$ as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:base-PR}
 v_{n+1} = (p E + (1 - p) P) v_n.
\end{equation}
Here $E \in \R^{N \times N}$ represents the uniform random distribution, so it is the $1/N$ times the all $1$ matrix.  However, note that this step can be implemented by replacing $p E v_n$ with a fixed vector $e \in \R^N$ that has $p/N$ in each coordinate.  

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Baseline Traffic PageRank Model}
\label{sec:baseline-pagerank}

Our baseline traffic model is to adopt this PageRank model to our large roughly $100,000$ ($N = 99,716$) link network.  Since our population data mapped to the road network links, these links map to the \emph{nodes} in the graph, not the edges.  The intersections (and direct connection between consecutive links) are the edges.  This switch (links $\neq$ edges) is slightly un-intuitive, but allows for the precise sort of modeling we seek, and aligns with our population date mapping to links.  

Then we can define the probability transition matrix $P \in \R^{N \times N}$ by the road network; we make the probability transition uniform $1/k$ among all ($k$) options from a directed link.  
Then with $E \in \R^{N \times N}$ with each entry $E_{i,j} = 1/N$ our transition update follows \eqref{eq:base-PR} precisely.  

In addition to this modeling being able to generate realistic routes, it can also generate global traffic distributions.  By iteratively applying \eqref{eq:base-PR}, the limit $\lim_{n \to \infty} v_n = v_{\star}$ provides the modeled distribution of traffic across all road network links.  

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Calibrating $p$ from the trip corpus.}
We next need to calibrate $p$ from the trip corpus.  
The chain in \eqref{eq:base-PR} is a geometric absorption
process: the number of graph steps before a teleport is
$\textsf{Geom}(p)$ on $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ with mean
$\frac{1 - p}{p}$. We match $p$ to the empirical mean trip length
$\mathbb{E}[K_{\textsf{total}}]$ by method-of-moments:
\[
p \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \mathbb{E}[K_{\textsf{total}}]}.
\]
On the map-matched corpus
($714{,}594$ trips, all route lengths), the empirical mean is
$\mathbb{E}[K_{\textsf{total}}] = 18.68$, which gives
$p = 0.0508$.  We thus use this for this $p = 0.0508$ going forward.  
\Cref{fig:single-phase-hist} shows the
empirical $K_{\textsf{total}}$ histogram with the
$\textsf{Geom}(p = 0.0508)$ reference overlaid, illustrating the nice geometric fit to true route length distribution.

\paragraph{Implementation Details.}
The sparse probability transition probability matrix $P$, which as with the map-matched observation matrix $C$, we represent as a sparse matrix.  It describes the directed road graph: each column of $P$ being uniform over the outgoing road neighbors of one link.  Then using a teleport
probability $p = 0.0508$, the iteration in \eqref{eq:base-PR} is applied until 
$\|v_{n+1} - v_n\|_1 < 10^{-7}$, 
with a safety cap of $n=300$ iterations. The cap is conservative: the power
iteration converges geometrically at rate of at least $1 - p$, so the
theoretical worst-case number of iterations to reach L1 error
$10^{-7}$ is
\[
n^{\star} \;=\; \frac{\ln 10^{-7}}{\ln (1 - p)}
\;=\; \frac{\ln 10^{-7}}{\ln 0.9492} \;\approx\; 310.
\]
We checked empirically on $1{,}500+$ random target bins and the chain converged before $300$ iterations
in every single case; the observed maximum was $254$ iterations and the median was $209$.

\subsection{PageRank Model with Informed Teleportation}
\label{sec:informed-tele}
Beyond this baseline model, the first modification we make is so that the teleportation step can be more informed than the default uniform.  That is, which links to start routes are not uniform.  Its quite rare to \emph{start} a route on a busy freeway, and it should be more likely to start one from a grocery store parking lot.  

For each time window $b$ we can track all routes that start in that period.  After normalizing these counts we can get a probability distribution $e_b \in \R^N$.  Like the traffic population matrix $F_b$, this also faces an extreme sparsity challenge.  So we apply the same graph diffusion method (as in Section \ref{sec:laplace}) to get a diffuse route initialization distribution $\tilde e_b$.  

Our first innovation replaces the uniform random teleportation matrix with the population diffusion model of routes $\tilde E_b$, where each column is $\tilde e_b$.  Then the simulation update step is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inf-PR}
v_{n+1} = (p \tilde E_b + (1 - p) P) v_n = p \tilde e_b + (1-p) P v_n.
\end{equation}
This accomplishes two important aspects.  First, it adapts the simulation to start routes at more realistic locations informed by actual data.  Second, allows the model to adapt to the specific time-window $b$; so the traffic model can generate different routes at different times during the day or week.

\subsection{PageRank Model with Learned Transitions}
\label{sec:learned-transitions}

We next apply machine learning to learn a more informed probability transition matrix $P$.  A potentially unrealistic part of a PageRank model is that the routes are random walks, while realistic routes are directed and meaningful.   However, the Markov chain modeling requires the \emph{Markov} properties that each step is memoryless, so we cannot use this entire set up if we inform an origin-destination pair.  However, we can adapt the transition probabilities to reflect more realistic to choices that model traffic.  
We do so with two modeling aspects following the principals that routes tend to move towards higher-throughput links.  

These aspects leverage physical throughput attributes of links $\ell$ of the graph: 
flow speed $s(\ell)$ and a lane count $\lambda(\ell)$. Let
$\bar s(\ell) = s(\ell)/\bar s$ and
$\bar \lambda(\ell) = \lambda(\ell)/\bar \lambda$ denote these
attributes normalized by their per-link mean over the network.  
%The third aspect captures how straight the transition is:  
%$a(\ell,\ell') = (1 + \cos \theta(\ell,\ell') + \delta)$ is the angular similarity between the directions of travel for the current link $\ell'$ and the potential next link $\ell$.  It is normalized so that if they are in the same direction $a(\ell,\ell') = 1$, if they are perpendicular, then $a(\ell,\ell') = 1/2$, and of they are the opposite direction then $a(\ell,\ell') = \delta$ (so a small U-turn probability is allowed; we set $\delta = 0.05$).  
%We similarly normalize this $\bar{a}(\ell,\ell') = a(\ell,\ell')/\bar a$ where $\bar a$ is the average over the network.  

Now given from a link $\ell_i$ the weight of each potential outgoing link $\ell_j$ is parameterized by $2$ parameters $\alpha_s$ and $\alpha_l$ so that
\[
w(j) = \bar s(\ell_j)^{\alpha_s}  \bar \lambda(\ell_j)^{\alpha_l}.
%\bar a(\ell_j,\ell_i)^{\alpha_a}.  
\]
Finally, we can set the transition probability matrix from $i$ to $j$ among all outgoing edges $J_i$ from $i$ as
\[
P_{j,i} = \frac{w(j)}{\sum_{j \in J_i} w(j)}.
\]
And $P$ retains its sparsity with $P_{j,i}=0$ for $j \notin J_i$.  

It remains to set the values for $\alpha = (\alpha_s, \alpha_l)$.  These values $\alpha_s, \alpha_l$ are larger when there is a preference to turn onto a link with higher throughput (because of higher flow-rate e.g., speed limit, or number of lanes).  A negative value in $\alpha_s, \alpha_l$ would indicate a preference to turn onto smaller roads.  
%Similarly, a large $\alpha_a$ value would indicate a preference to stay on straighter roads; which we expect for more purposeful driving routes.  

Towards determining these, we set up a loss function 
\[
\mathcal{L}(\alpha) = \sum_{b \in [T]} \sum_{i \in [N]} \frac{| v_\star(\tilde E_b, \alpha)[i] - \tilde F_b[i]| }{\tilde F_b[i] + \varepsilon}
\]
where $v_\star(\tilde E_b, \alpha)$ is the converged-to PageRank vector for a Markov chain following \eqref{eq:inf-PR}, where $P$ is defined using the specified $\alpha$ parameters.  
Given the diffused starting positions $\tilde E_b$, the goal is to match the diffused observed traffic population $\tilde F_b$.  
It is the MRE (mean relative error), summed over all time periods $b$; thus the goal is to set these $\alpha$ parameters globally for all timescales; that is to learn the driving preferences for the metro area regardless of the time of day.    While it may be the case that these driving patterns change throughout the day or week, we opt to learn a smaller set of parameters with goal of not overfitting.  Moreover, this training step is somewhat computationally expensive, and repeating it 8000+ times, would be exorbitant.  

Finally, we can choose these parameters $\alpha^* = \arg \min_{\alpha} \mathcal{L}(\alpha)$.  We do this with gradient descent.  We use \texttt{autograd} to determine the gradient, and try several random restarts to confirm it reliably finds the same minimal parameter choices.

\subsection{Two-phase {PageRank} Model}
\label{sec:2p-PR}

The final aspect of our model is based on the observation that most traffic routes gradually move to higher throughout links from the origin, but the destination is usually not on these high throughput links.  As it approaches a destination, the route tends to move towards lower throughput links.  However, the memoryless aspect of PageRank models does not directly allow for a goal destination or these two phases.  

Towards addressing this, we describe a two-phase PageRank model.  Instead of a $N$ state model, this becomes a $2N$ state model, where each link can be visited in the \emph{up} phase as the route tends to move towards higher throughput links as it leave the origin, or in the \emph{down} phase as the route tends to move towards lower throughput links as it approaches the destination.  
The up phase is now governed by a probability transition matrix $P^\uparrow$ with parameters $\alpha^\uparrow = (\alpha_s^\uparrow, \alpha_l^\uparrow)$, and the down phase by a separate $P^\downarrow$ with its own parameters $\alpha^\downarrow = (\alpha_s^\downarrow, \alpha_l^\downarrow)$.  
Then instead of a single parameter $p$ to govern the probability the route ends, we need two parameters: $\beta$ and  $\rho$.  The $\beta$ parameter is the probability that when in the up phase the route transitions to the down phase, and $\rho$ parameter is the probability that when in the down phase the route reaches its destination and ends.  While after the down phase ends, the route needs to teleport to a new starting spot (based on $\tilde E_b$), when the up phase ends the route should stay on the same link as before.  We can capture this entire in a transition probability matrix $M_b \in \R^{2N \times 2N}$, where each component is an $N \times N$ block:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:M-block}
M_b \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
(1 - \beta)\, P^{\uparrow} & \rho\, \tilde E_b\, \mathbf{1}^{\top} \\[6pt]
\beta\, I                  & (1 - \rho)\, P^{\downarrow}
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the $N \times N$ identity and $\mathbf{1} \in
\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is the all-ones column vector. 
With this convention the Markov chain's mass distribution
$v \in \mathbb{R}^{2N}$ evolves as the simple matrix-vector product (which can be still implemented block-wise with efficient sparse operations):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:2P-iter}
v_{n+1} \;=\; M_b\, v_n.  
\end{equation}

Note that since state distribution $v_n$ and the converged to $v_\star$ is now $2N$-dimensional, in order to compare to a desired diffused population state $\tilde F_b \in \R^N$, we need to ``fold" $v_\star$ into $\R^{2N}$.  That is let $\hat v_\star \in \R^N$ be defined for index $i \in [N]$ as $\hat v_\star[i] = v_\star[i] + v_\star[N+i]$.  That is we add together the probabilities of being in the up state $i^\uparrow = i$ and the down state $i^\downarrow = N+i$.

\paragraph{Calibrating $\beta$ and $\rho$.}
\label{sec:beta-rho-calibration}

The two scalars $\beta$ and $\rho$ control how long the chain
stays in each phase before switching.  However, the sum of two geometric distributions follows a negative binomial distribution which does not match the observations in Figure \ref{fig:single-phase-hist}; the negative binomial will have peak probability away from $0$.  
We would rather like to interpret their ideal values as
$\beta = \frac{1}{1 + \mathbb{E}[L_{\uparrow}]}$ and
$\rho = \frac{1}{1 + \mathbb{E}[L_{\downarrow}]}$, where
$L_{\uparrow}$ and $L_{\downarrow}$ are the empirical up- and
down-phase path lengths.  To do so, we need to model the lengths of the up and the down phase separately from the corpus.  

%The two scalars are not tuned against the evaluation metric; they are read off the empirical distribution of trip lengths in the matched-route corpus, in three steps.

%\paragraph{Per-trip peak split.}

\paragraph{Fitting Two-Phase PageRank Model}
Finally, to complete the two-phase PageRank model, we need to fit the parameters $\alpha^\uparrow$ and $\alpha^\downarrow$ for the full model to be complete.  %However, we expect the dependence on the angular similarity $\alpha_a$ to not depend on the phase of the model, so we set $\alpha_a^\uparrow = \alpha_a^\downarrow$.  
%Moreover, we simplify so the model is invertible, and the proclivity to favor links with larger throughput parameters going up should be the opposite of that of going down to destination.  So we also set $\alpha_s^\uparrow = - \alpha_s^\downarrow$ and $\alpha_l^\uparrow = - \alpha_l^\downarrow$.  As a result we are back to only three paramaters $\alpha = \alpha^\uparrow = (\alpha_s^\uparrow, \alpha_l^\uparrow, \alpha_a^\uparrow)$ to learn.  
As in the single phase model, we set up a loss function 
\[
\mathcal{L}(\alpha) = \sum_{b \in [T]} \sum_{i \in [N]} \frac{| \hat v_\star(\tilde E_b, \alpha)[i] - \tilde F_b[i]| }{\tilde F_b[i] + \varepsilon}
\]
where $\hat v_\star(\tilde E_b, \alpha) \in \R^N$ now the folded, converged-to two-phase PageRank vector for a Markov chain following \eqref{eq:2P-iter}, where $P^\uparrow$ and $P^\downarrow$ are defined with the specified $\alpha^\uparrow$ and $\alpha^\downarrow$ parameters where now $\alpha = (\alpha^\uparrow,\alpha^\downarrow) = (\alpha_s^\uparrow,\alpha_l^\uparrow,\alpha_s^\downarrow,\alpha_l^\downarrow) \in \R^4$.  
Given the diffused starting positions $\tilde E_b$, the goal is still to match the diffused observed traffic population $\tilde F_b$.  As before we choose these parameters $\alpha* = \arg \min_{\alpha \in \R^4} \mathcal{L}(\alpha)$.  We do this with gradient descent via \texttt{autograd}.

\section{Global Forecasting Evaluation}
\label{sec:global-forecast}

We now evaluate our model on how well it predicts the global traffic distribution over the course of a week.  
We use MRE as described in Section \ref{sec:evaluation-framework}.    More specifically, we will aim to evaluate the model using the last 7 days of data.  We report the Ov.MRE in three ways:  
the \emph{train} setting evaluates on the first 23 days that are used to train $\alpha$;
the \emph{test} setting evaluates on the last 7 days, held out from the training of $\alpha$, but this still uses the diffused starting point distribution $\tilde E_b$ to define teleportation for the same time period $b$; and 
the \emph{forecast} also evaluates on the last 7 days, but uses the diffused starting point distribution $\tilde E_{b-7d}$ from precisely 7 days prior; but against the diffused traffic population $\tilde F_b$ in the last 7 days.  
The first case train does not maintain a test/train split, and is shown for completeness.   The test evaluation does maintain a test train split to evaluate the choices of $\alpha$, but information may leak from the diffused starting points $\tilde E_b$ (which are different from the diffused traffic population $\tilde F_b$). Finally, the forecast version keeps all data from training part separate from the evaluation.  

% We compute the average MRE value of the model $\hat v_\star(\tilde E_{b-7d}, \alpha)$ against the diffused data $\tilde F_b$.  Note that the index of $\tilde E_{b-7d}$ indicates it is the diffused observation of starting routes from precisely 7 days earlier in time then the ones evaluated $\tilde F_b$.  However, the parameters $\alpha$ are learned using, averaged over the first 23 days of observations.  This maintains a train-test split.  
To be clear, in the forecast setting, 
we compute the average relative error for each link $i \in [N]$ for every $b$ in the last week as 
        \[
          \mathrm{MRE}_b
          \;=\;
          \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
          \frac{\bigl|\tilde F_{b,i} - v_\star(\tilde E_{b-7d},\alpha)\bigr|}
               {\tilde F_{b,i} + \varepsilon}.
        \]
The Overall MRE (Ov.MRE) is reported by considering $5$ minute time bins $b \in [T_{-7d}]$ for $T_{-7d} = [2018$-$09$-$23\ 19{:}00,\ 2018$-$09$-$30\ 18{:}55]$ which are in the last week over observations, held out for evaluation.  That is we compute and report
\[
\mathrm{Ov.MRE}
 = 
 \frac{1}{T_{-7d}} \sum_{b \in [T_{-7d}]} \mathrm{MRE}_b.
\]

\section{Event Modeling}
\label{sec:event-modeling}

We show how to apply this framework to events that have predictable configuration changes, which allows for updated modeling.  

There are two direct ways we can leverage our model.   
First, if there are road closures (either planned or unplanned), then we can perform a simple modification of the graph by removing those links.  
Second, if there is a large event attracting many of additional people to a small area, then we can adapt the route origin distribution $\tilde E_b$ for the specified time windows $b$ for the event.  In practice for sporting events or festivals we can use prior events for these modified distribution estimates, but because we only have access to one month of data, we use a training subset of the data from the $\tilde E_b$ data actual event.  
%To build a forecast, we train how to increase $\tilde E_b$ on half of the links, and measure generalization on the other half.  
In both cases, we do not need to retrain the $\alpha$ parameters, only to rerun the PageRank iteration \eqref{eq:2P-iter}; which is fast because we maintain a sparse format, and the distribution vector $v_\star$ is converged to quickly.  

Moreover, on a metro scale network, even large events have relatively local effect.  As a result, we change the model prediction and evaluate their effect on local region around the events (in addition to the global evaluation -- which barely changes).

\subsection{Case study 2: Large Sporting Event}
\label{sec:stadium}

A Saturday football game at Rice-Eccles Stadium (Washington at Utah,
2018-09-15, kickoff 20:00, attendance 47{,}445) is a pure demand event:
it closes no road, but it concentrates trips around the venue. We therefore
keep the full network and intervene only on the prior. We score on an
evaluation box centered on the stadium (Fig.~\ref{fig:stadium-map};
$1{,}367$ links) over the $12$ five-minute bins from 23:00 to 23:55, the
end-of-game departure window (the game ended at 23:19), comparing the
chain output to the game-day diffused traffic population. The fed prior is
the previous week's diffused origins (the clean Saturday 2018-09-08, same
hours).
%both chains use the speed and lane exponents (single phase $\alpha=0.0508$; two-phase up/down $\beta=0.102$, $\rho=0.101$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/rice_eccles_event_links_GPT.pdf}
\caption{Rice-Eccles study area: the evaluation box (dashed) and the
stadium (marker); no links are closed.}
\label{fig:stadium-map}
\end{figure}

Normally, we would learn the expected traffic from similar prior events, but without that in the dataset, we perform a test train split on the links within the evaluation box, and learn the increase in traffic population that way.
To do so, we partition all links into the
evaluation box $B$ and its complement $A$, with prior masses
$a=\sum_{x\in A}p(x)$ and $b=\sum_{x\in B}p(x)$, $a+b=1$. We move a traffic probability
$\psi$ from $A$ into $B$:
\[
q(x)=p(x)\left(1-\frac{\psi}{a}\right)\ \ (x\in A),
\qquad
q(x)=p(x)\left(1+\frac{\psi}{b}\right)\ \ (x\in B).
\]
This keeps $q$ a probability distribution ($\sum_x q(x)=1$) and stays
non-negative for $-b<\psi<a$. A positive $\psi$ concentrates the fed
demand on the stadium box, a negative $\psi$ thins it, and $\psi=0$
recovers the unmodified previous-week prior; the shape within each block
is unchanged, only the box's share of the mass moves.

We treat $\psi$ as the single free parameter and choose it to minimize the
eval-box mean squared error on a training portion of the data, then report
the error on a held-out portion. We validate it over the $1{,}367$
\emph{box links} (random $75\%$ train, $25\%$ test), with a fixed seed; the
single-phase and two-phase models are fit independently.

Table~\ref{tab:stadium-psi} gives the learned $\psi^\star$ and the
held-out box mean squared error. Both models learn a positive transfer
($\psi^\star\approx+0.012$) that concentrates the fed demand on the stadium
box, and it lowers the held-out box squared error below the unmodified
previous-week prior. A single, data-light scalar fit on a subset of links
thus transfers cleanly to the held-out remainder.

\subsection{Case study 3: Street festival}
\label{sec:festival}

The 9th \& 9th Street Festival (Saturday 2018-09-15, 10:00 to 17:55)
closes 900~South and 900~East at the 9th-and-9th intersection, $14$ links
in all (Fig.~\ref{fig:festival-map}). Unlike the stadium, the festival
removes links, so we first apply surgery (as with the crash,
\S\ref{sec:crash}): we remove the $14$ closed links from the network, zero
them out of the prior, and redistribute their popularity onto the
evaluation box, re-solving the chain on the open network. On top of
surgery we intervene on the prior. As in the stadium study
(\S\ref{sec:stadium}), we feed the previous week's diffused origins (the
clean Saturday 2018-09-08, same hours) and score on the evaluation box
(Fig.~\ref{fig:festival-map}; $694$ open links) over the $12$ five-minute
bins from 17:00 to 17:55, the closing hour of the festival, comparing the
chain output to the festival-day diffused traffic population.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/festival_event_links_GPT.pdf}
\caption{Street festival study area: the $14$ closed links (red), the
evaluation box (dashed), and the same area on the basemap.}
\label{fig:festival-map}
\end{figure}

We apply the prior mass-transfer $\psi$ of \S\ref{sec:stadium} on top of
surgery, moving a traffic probability $\psi$ from the open complement into
the evaluation box. We treat $\psi$ as the single free parameter and
choose it to minimize the eval-box mean squared error on a training
portion of the box links (random $75\%$ train, $25\%$ test, fixed seed),
then report the error on the held-out portion; the single-phase and
two-phase models are fit independently.

Table~\ref{tab:festival-psi} gives the learned $\psi^\star$ and the
held-out box mean squared error. Both models learn a positive transfer
($\psi^\star\approx+0.005$) that concentrates the fed demand on the
festival box, and on top of surgery it lowers the held-out box squared
error below the unmodified prior. A single, data-light scalar fit on a
subset of links thus transfers cleanly to the held-out remainder.

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclude}

We show how to build a metro-scale traffic model at the link level, at 5-minute resolution, from large yet extremely sparse data.  Such data is very noisy week to week, and overcoming the noise and sparsity is a significant challenge.  

A key feature of our model is that it is a learned PageRank model, that is optimized to the training data.  
This means that the model can not only easily generate full routes, but also quickly provides full metro-wide traffic population estimates.  Competing methods do not offer this level of resolution for the routes, or do not allow for directly inferred population predictions.  

Through a combination of realistic traffic smoothing and careful modeling, we produce estimates that are plausible and significantly better than the raw measurement distributions.  We believe this underexplored modeling at this scale offers a lot of promise for metro-scale foundation models for traffic and mobility.

Table \ref{tab:gamma-zero-cells} shows similar statistics for the cells with initial counts of $0$. In this case it only makes sense to use the global noise floor $\NFG$.  
The median
and $95$th-percentile ratios are essentially constant at
$\approx 4 \times 10^{-4}$ across all $\gamma$: at least $95\%$
of zero cells never see diffusion mass, they only carry the
Laplace floor $\alpha / N$. The fraction of zero cells exceeding
the half-noise tolerance is $0.08\%$ at $\gamma = 0.20$
($\sim 1.5 \times 10^{5}$ cells out of $1.95 \times 10^{8}$);
these are the cells immediately adjacent to active links and
they are the ones that get filled in. The takeaway is
that the diffusion is bounded even on the cells where the
per-cell criterion of \cref{tab:gamma-noise-floor} is undefined:
it lifts a small adjacency-defined tail of zero cells into the
noise band and leaves the rest at the Laplace floor.

\begin{abstract}
We present a comprehensive derivation, analysis, and empirical evaluation of the
\emph{Two-Phase PageRank with Travel-Time Self-Loops} algorithm, developed to predict
macroscopic traffic congestion on the Salt Lake City road network (99{,}716 distinct road
links). Standard PageRank suffers from a fundamental physical defect: it treats every
link transition as instantaneous, causing excessive probability-mass diffusion and a Mean
Relative Error (MRE) of \textbf{0.7331} on the busiest 100 links.

We resolve this by injecting physical dwell time into the transition matrix via
\emph{self-loop weights} proportional to a link's traversal time, controlled by a single
global parameter $\mu$. We further split the network into two phases---active driving and
local dispersal---forming a $2N\!\times\!2N$ block transition matrix. With optimal
parameters ($\mu=20,\;\beta=0.90,\;d=0.80$) the Top-100 MRE plunges to
\textbf{0.0317}---a \textbf{95.7\%} improvement---while the overall MRE simultaneously
falls to \textbf{0.0471} (76.2\% improvement), demonstrating that the fix is physically
principled rather than over-fitted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
%=============================================================

\subsection{The Traffic Prediction Problem}

Modern cities generate massive streams of GPS trajectories from vehicles, ride-sharing
apps, navigation systems, and fleet tracking. The challenge is to convert a \emph{sparse
sample} of these trajectories---typically covering only a small fraction of the
network---into a reliable estimate of macroscopic traffic volume across \emph{every} road
segment.

For Salt Lake City, the road network has $N = 99{,}716$ distinct directed road links.
In a given 15-minute window, raw GPS data may only directly observe traffic on a few
thousand of these links. The prediction question is: given this partial view, what is the
full traffic distribution?

\subsection{Why PageRank?}

PageRank models network flow as a random walk over a directed graph. A random walker
(simulated vehicle) either moves to one of the current link's successors or teleports to
a node sampled from a \emph{teleportation distribution}. The stationary distribution of
this Markov chain represents the predicted fraction of time (and thus traffic) on each
link.

The key advantage of PageRank is that it naturally propagates observed traffic through
the network topology: high traffic on one road \emph{implies} high traffic on connected
feeders and exits, even if those roads were not directly observed.

\subsection{The Fundamental Defect: Instantaneous Transitions}

\begin{warning}
  Standard PageRank treats every link transition as taking the same mathematical
  ``time step''. A vehicle transitioning from a 1\,km highway at 100\,km/h and a
  vehicle transitioning through a 10\,m intersection connector at 20\,km/h both
  advance in exactly one iteration. This is physically absurd.
\end{warning}

Because the highway segment takes $t_{\text{highway}} = 1000/27.8 \approx 36$\,s to
traverse while the connector takes $t_{\text{connector}} = 10/5.6 \approx 1.8$\,s, the
highway vehicle realistically spends $\sim\!20\times$ longer at that location.
Standard PageRank ignores this completely, causing excessive \emph{diffusion}: traffic
flows through long roads too quickly and spreads uniformly, washing out the concentration
peaks on major arterial roads.

\section{Data Pre-Processing: Spatial Smoothing}
%=============================================================

\subsection{The Sparsity Problem}

The raw trajectory data is converted into a vehicle-count vector $\mathbf{c} \in
\mathbb{R}^N$, where $c_i$ counts the number of GPS-traced vehicles observed on link
$i$ during the evaluation window. Because only a tiny fraction of roads are directly
observed, the vast majority of entries are $c_i = 0$.

\begin{warning}
  A zero-valued entry $c_i = 0$ creates a division-by-zero singularity in the Mean
  Relative Error metric, and it causes PageRank's teleportation vector to have a vast
  ``dead zone'' that absorbs probability mass and prevents natural diffusion. Empirically,
  using raw counts gives an overall MRE exceeding $7000$.
\end{warning}

\subsection{Laplace Pseudo-Count}

To avoid zero denominators, we apply a small Laplace smoothing constant
$\alpha = 0.1$ before any further processing:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{c}_i = c_i + \alpha, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, N.
\end{equation}
This ensures every link has a strictly positive prior probability.

\subsection{Graph-Diffusion Smoothing}

The smoothed count vector is then diffused along the road network adjacency structure.
Let $\mathbf{A} \in \{0,1\}^{N \times N}$ be the adjacency matrix of the road graph
(with $A_{ij}=1$ if link $i$ connects to link $j$), and let $\mathbf{P}_{\text{adj}}$
be its row-stochastic normalisation:
\begin{equation}
  {P_{\text{adj}}}_{ij} = \frac{A_{ij}}{\sum_{k} A_{ik}}.
\end{equation}

Graph-diffusion smoothing applies a convex mixture of the raw signal and its
one-hop neighbourhood average:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\mathbf{c}_{\text{smooth}} = (1 - \gamma)\,\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
    + \gamma\,\mathbf{P}_{\text{adj}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}
  \label{eq:smoothing}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the diffusion factor.

Equation~(\ref{eq:smoothing}) is applied for a single step. The result is then
$L^1$-normalised to obtain the teleportation vector:
\begin{equation}
  E_N[i] = \frac{c_{\text{smooth},i}}{\sum_{j=1}^N c_{\text{smooth},j}}, \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^N E_N[i] = 1.
\end{equation}

\begin{insight}
  Increasing $\gamma$ ``borrows'' probability mass from adjacent well-observed roads and
  spreads it to unobserved neighbours. This reduces variance (noisy isolated spikes) but
  introduces bias (we must not over-smooth genuine traffic peaks).
\end{insight}

\subsection{Optimal Smoothing Parameter via Cross-Validation}

To find the best $\gamma$, we performed a Monte Carlo Cross-Validation over 49 distinct
15-minute timeframes. For each timeframe, the GPS traces were split into two halves
(Set~A and Set~B). We apply smoothing only to Set~A, then measure Mean Squared Error
against the raw Set~B counts as the proxy ground truth.

  The minimum held-out MSE is achieved at $\gamma^{*} = 0.26$. Using this value

\end{result}

\section{The Core Physical Fix: Travel-Time Self-Loops}
%=============================================================

\subsection{Mathematical Definition}

For each link $i$ with physical length $\ell_i$ (metres) and free-flow speed $s_i$
(m/s), define the \emph{traversal time}:
\begin{equation}
  t_i = \frac{\ell_i}{s_i} \quad [\text{seconds}].
\end{equation}

We introduce a global \emph{dwell-time scaling parameter} $\mu > 0$ and define the
\emph{self-loop weight} of link $i$ as:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\sw_i = \mu \cdot t_i = \mu \cdot \frac{\ell_i}{s_i}}.
  \label{eq:selfloop}
\end{equation}

Let $w_{ij}$ be the weight of the forward edge from link $i$ to successor link $j$.
In the base configuration ($\alpha_s = \alpha_l = 0$), $w_{ij} = 1$ for all valid
outgoing edges. The \emph{total weight} leaving link $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \TW_i = \sw_i + \sum_{j \in \operatorname{succ}(i)} w_{ij}
         = \mu t_i + |\operatorname{succ}(i)|.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Normalised Transition Matrix $P$}

The $N \times N$ transition matrix $P$ is constructed as:
\begin{align}
  P_{ii} &= \frac{\sw_i}{\TW_i} = \frac{\mu t_i}{\mu t_i + |\operatorname{succ}(i)|}
  & \text{(self-loop probability)},\\[6pt]
  P_{ij} &= \frac{w_{ij}}{\TW_i} = \frac{1}{\mu t_i + |\operatorname{succ}(i)|}
  & \text{(forward probability, } j \in \operatorname{succ}(i)\text{)},\\[6pt]
  P_{ij} &= 0 & \text{(otherwise)}.
\end{align}

By construction, every row of $P$ sums to 1, making $P$ a valid row-stochastic matrix.

\subsection{Worked Examples}

\begin{example}[Highway Segment]
  \label{ex:highway}
  Consider a highway segment with parameters: $\ell_i = 500$\,m, $s_i = 25$\,m/s,
  3 successors, $\mu = 20$.

  \begin{align*}
    t_i &= 500 / 25 = 20\text{ s}\\
    \sw_i &= 20 \times 20 = 400\\
    \TW_i &= 400 + 3 = 403\\[4pt]
    P_{ii} &= 400 / 403 \approx 0.9926 \quad \textbf{(99.26\% stays per step)}\\
    P_{ij} &= 1   / 403 \approx 0.0025 \quad \text{per successor}
  \end{align*}

  After $k=100$ iterations:
  $\; (0.9926)^{100} \approx 0.473$ --- the highway retains nearly half its initial mass.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Short Connector]
  \label{ex:connector}
  Consider $\ell_i = 50$\,m, $s_i = 10$\,m/s, 3 successors, $\mu = 20$.

  \begin{align*}
    t_i &= 50 / 10 = 5\text{ s}\\
    \sw_i &= 20 \times 5 = 100\\
    \TW_i &= 100 + 3 = 103\\[4pt]
    P_{ii} &= 100 / 103 \approx 0.9709 \quad \text{(97.09\% stays per step)}\\
    P_{ij} &= 1   / 103 \approx 0.0097 \quad \text{per successor}
  \end{align*}

  After $k=100$ iterations: $(0.9709)^{100} \approx 0.053$ ---
  the connector has shed $\sim\!95\%$ of its initial mass.
\end{example}

\begin{insight}
  Over 100 iterations, the highway retains $\mathbf{47\%}$ of its initial mass while the
  connector retains only $\mathbf{5\%}$.  This directly models the physical reality that
  vehicles spend $\frac{500/25}{50/10} = 4\times$ longer on the highway than on the
  connector under $\mu\!=\!20$, leading to a natural traffic accumulation without any
  ad-hoc forcing.
\end{insight}

\subsection{Self-Loop Probability as a Function of Traversal Time}

\begin{proposition}
  For fixed $\mu$ and $k$ successors, the self-loop probability $P_{ii}$ is a
  monotonically increasing function of $t_i$:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{ii}(t_i) = \frac{\mu t_i}{\mu t_i + k}
    \xrightarrow{t_i \to \infty} 1, \qquad
    P_{ii}(0) = 0.
    \label{eq:pii_formula}
  \end{equation}
  Furthermore, the link's effective ``time constant'' under repeated application is:
  \begin{equation}
    \tau_i = -\frac{1}{\ln P_{ii}} \approx \frac{\mu t_i}{k}
    \quad \text{(for } \mu t_i \gg k\text{)}.
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\section{The Two-Phase Network: $2N \times 2N$ Block Matrix}
%=============================================================

\subsection{Motivation for Two Phases}

Traffic is inherently directional during active driving. However, after arriving at a
destination, vehicles park and disperse locally---they no longer follow the directed
highway graph. Modelling both behaviours in a single matrix would conflate forward-flow
dynamics with local dispersal and obscure the physical interpretation.

We therefore duplicate the $N$-node network into two identical \emph{phases}:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Up Phase} ($i = 1, \ldots, N$): vehicles actively driving, following
    the directed road graph with self-loops.
  \item \textbf{Down Phase} ($i = N+1, \ldots, 2N$): vehicles parked or dispersing
    locally at reduced speed; they use the same transition matrix $P$ but can never
    re-enter the Up phase.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Block Matrix Construction}

A parameter $\beta \in [0,1]$ controls how quickly mass ``falls'' from the Up phase to
the Down phase at each iteration. The full $2N \times 2N$ transition matrix is:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{M_{2N} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    (1-\beta)\,P & \beta\,I_N \\
    \mathbf{0}_N & P
  \end{bmatrix}}
  \label{eq:block}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $I_N$ is the $N \times N$ identity matrix and $\mathbf{0}_N$ is the
$N \times N$ zero matrix.

\subsection{Interpretation of Each Block}

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Semantic interpretation of each $N\!\times\!N$ block of $M_{2N}$.}
  \label{tab:blocks}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{8cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Block & Expression & Physical meaning\\
    \midrule
    Top-Left    & $(1-\beta)P$   & Probability of staying in the Up phase and
                                   moving (or self-looping) according to $P$.\\
    Top-Right   & $\beta I_N$    & Probability of dropping from Up phase to the
                                   \emph{same} link in the Down phase (vertical
                                   transition, identity preserves location).\\
    Bottom-Left & $\mathbf{0}_N$ & Down-to-Up transition is \emph{impossible}: once
                                   parked, a vehicle cannot re-enter active flow.\\
    Bottom-Right& $P$            & Down-phase vehicles continue their local random
                                   walk according to $P$ (with self-loops).\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Row-Stochasticity of $M_{2N}$}

For any row $i$ in the Up phase (top half):
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j} [M_{2N}]_{i,j}
  = (1-\beta)\underbrace{\sum_j P_{ij}}_{=1} + \beta \cdot 1 = 1. \checkmark
\end{equation}
For any row $i$ in the Down phase (bottom half):
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j} [M_{2N}]_{i,j} = \sum_j P_{ij} = 1. \checkmark
\end{equation}
Hence $M_{2N}$ is row-stochastic and defines a valid Markov chain over $2N$ states.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{fig5_block_matrix.png}
  \caption{Visual structure of the $2N \times 2N$ block transition matrix.
    The bottom-left block is identically zero, enforcing the one-way phase transition.}
  \label{fig:block}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Effect of $\beta$ on Phase Dynamics}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta = 0$: All mass remains in the Up phase indefinitely; the two-phase
    structure degenerates to standard single-phase PageRank.
  \item $\beta = 1$: Every vehicle falls to the Down phase after exactly one step;
    no forward active-driving evolution occurs.
  \item $\beta = 0.90$ (optimal): Vehicles transition to the Down phase after roughly
    one hop, preventing excessive forward diffusion while self-loops handle dwell time.
\end{itemize}

At the stationary distribution, mass is shared between phases. For $\beta \to 1$, nearly
all mass accumulates in the Down phase where it continues to evolve under $P$.

%=============================================================
\section{The Teleportation Vector $E_{2N}$}
%=============================================================

\subsection{Role of Teleportation in PageRank}

In the standard PageRank update rule, with probability $(1-d)$ a walker teleports to a
random location instead of following the transition matrix. This teleportation serves two
purposes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ergodicity}: guarantees the Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic,
    ensuring a unique stationary distribution.
  \item \textbf{Bias injection}: instead of teleporting uniformly, we bias toward our
    empirical ground-truth observation vector $E_N$, injecting observed traffic data into
    the simulation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Constructing $E_{2N}$}

The smoothed normalised traffic vector $E_N \in \mathbb{R}^N$ (see Section~2) is the
teleportation distribution over the physical $N$ links.

Since teleportation represents the ``birth'' of a new simulated vehicle, all newly
injected vehicles should start in the \emph{Up phase} (actively driving). Therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{E_{2N} = \begin{bmatrix} E_N \\ \mathbf{0}_N \end{bmatrix}}
  \in \mathbb{R}^{2N}, \qquad \norm{E_{2N}}_1 = 1.
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
  Injecting mass only into the Up phase is physically motivated: vehicles ``start''
  their journey on a road in active mode. They will naturally transition to the Down phase
  as the simulation progresses.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Deprecated Enhancement Parameters}

In earlier algorithm versions, two additional parameters modified $E_N$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tau} ($\tau$): raised each $E_N$ entry to the power $\tau$ before
    renormalising, concentrating teleportation mass on high-traffic links.
  \item \textbf{Top-K Boost}: multiplied the entries of the top-$k$ busiest links by a
    factor, further concentrating mass.
\end{itemize}

Both approaches improved Top-100 MRE only by ``hacking'' the solution rather than fixing
the underlying physics. After the $\mu$ self-loop fix, $\tau=1.0$ and
$\text{top-k-boost}=1.0$, meaning $E_N$ is used in its pure, unmodified form.

%=============================================================
\section{The Power Iteration Algorithm}
%=============================================================

\subsection{PageRank Update Rule}

Let $v^{(k)}_{2N} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N}$ be the probability distribution over the $2N$
states at iteration $k$, with $\norm{v^{(k)}_{2N}}_1 = 1$. The PageRank update is:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{v^{(k+1)}_{2N} = d \cdot M_{2N}^{\top}\, v^{(k)}_{2N}
    + (1-d)\cdot E_{2N}}
  \label{eq:pagerank}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $d \in (0,1)$ is the \emph{damping factor}. With probability $d$, mass follows
    the transition dynamics; with probability $(1-d)$, mass teleports.
  \item $M_{2N}^{\top}$ is the transpose because we represent distributions as
    \emph{column vectors} and the transition matrix is row-stochastic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expanded Form}

Writing $v^{(k)} = \left[\vup^{(k)};\;\vdown^{(k)}\right]$ and substituting
(\ref{eq:block}):
\begin{align}
  \vup^{(k+1)} &= d\,(1-\beta)\,P^{\top}\,\vup^{(k)}
    + (1-d)\,E_N \label{eq:up}\\
  \vdown^{(k+1)} &= d\,\beta\,\vup^{(k)}
    + d\,P^{\top}\,\vdown^{(k)} \label{eq:down}
\end{align}

Equation~(\ref{eq:up}) shows that the Up-phase distribution evolves under scaled $P$
with teleportation injection. Equation~(\ref{eq:down}) shows the Down phase receiving a
``drip'' of $\beta$-fraction of the Up phase mass each iteration, while also evolving
under $P$.

\subsection{Pseudocode}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{Algorithm: Two-Phase PageRank with Travel-Time Self-Loops}
\smallskip\hrule\smallskip
\textbf{Input:} Graph $G$, smoothed distribution $E_N$, parameters $\mu,\beta,d$\\
\textbf{Output:} Predicted traffic $\vf\in\mathbb{R}^N$
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item Build $N\!\times\!N$ transition matrix $P$ using self-loop weights
        $\sw_i = \mu\cdot\ell_i/s_i$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:selfloop})
  \item Build $2N\!\times\!2N$ block matrix $M_{2N}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:block})
  \item Set $E_{2N}\leftarrow[E_N;\,\mathbf{0}_N]$; initialise $v\leftarrow E_{2N}$
  \item \textbf{Repeat:}
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
          \item $v_{\text{prev}}\leftarrow v$
          \item $v\leftarrow d\cdot M_{2N}^{\top}\,v + (1-d)\cdot E_{2N}$
        \end{enumerate}
        \phantom{\textbf{Repeat: }} \textbf{Until} $\|v-v_{\text{prev}}\|_1<10^{-6}$
  \item $\vup\leftarrow v[1:N]$,\quad $\vdown\leftarrow v[N+1:2N]$
  \item $\vf\leftarrow (\vup+\vdown)\,/\,\|\vup+\vdown\|_1$
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\caption{Pseudocode for the Two-Phase PageRank power iteration.}
\label{alg:pagerank}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}

The power iteration converges geometrically at rate $d \cdot \lambda_2(M_{2N})$, where
$\lambda_2$ is the second-largest eigenvalue of $M_{2N}$. The convergence bound after
$k$ iterations is:
\begin{equation}
  \norm{v^{(k)} - v^*}_1 \leq \left(d \cdot \lambda_2\right)^k \norm{v^{(0)} - v^*}_1.
\end{equation}

In practice, with $d = 0.80$ and our self-loop structure, convergence to $10^{-6}$
tolerance requires approximately 100--150 iterations for $N = 99{,}716$ on the SLC
network.

\end{figure}

\section{Extracting the Final Traffic Distribution}
%=============================================================

After convergence we have a $2N$-dimensional stationary vector $v^* =
[\vup^*;\;\vdown^*]$. The predicted traffic on physical link $i$ is the total probability
mass across both phases:
\begin{equation}
  \vf[i] = \vup^*[i] + \vdown^*[i], \qquad i = 1, \ldots, N.
\end{equation}

We then renormalise to a proper probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \vf \leftarrow \frac{\vf}{\norm{\vf}_1}.
\end{equation}

This is compared against the normalised ground-truth popularity $p_i$ (from smoothed
GPS counts) using the \emph{Mean Relative Error} (MRE):
\begin{equation}
  \text{MRE} = \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{i \in S} \frac{|\vf[i] - p_i|}{p_i},
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the evaluation set (either all $N$ links for ``Overall MRE'', or the 100
links with highest $p_i$ for ``Top-100 MRE'').

\section{Physical Interpretation and Theoretical Connections}
%=============================================================

\subsection{Connection to Continuous-Time Markov Chains}

Consider a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) where the holding time at state $i$ is
exponentially distributed with rate $\lambda_i$. In the embedded discrete-time chain,
the probability of transitioning to successor $j$ is $q_{ij} / \lambda_i$.

Our discrete self-loop construction approximates this with:
\begin{equation}
  P_{ii} = \frac{\mu t_i}{\mu t_i + k} \approx 1 - \frac{k}{\mu t_i}
  \qquad \text{for } \mu t_i \gg k.
\end{equation}

The effective ``exit rate'' per step is $\approx k/(\mu t_i)$, so the effective
holding time (in iterations) is $\approx \mu t_i / k$. This is proportional to the
physical traversal time $t_i$, achieving the desired CTMC approximation.

\subsection{Why $\mu=20$ Is Near-Optimal}

For a typical SLC highway ($t_i \approx 36$\,s, $k \approx 3$ successors):
\begin{equation}
  P_{ii} = \frac{20 \times 36}{20 \times 36 + 3} = \frac{720}{723} \approx 0.9959.
\end{equation}

For a typical connector ($t_i \approx 2$\,s):
\begin{equation}
  P_{ii} = \frac{20 \times 2}{20 \times 2 + 3} = \frac{40}{43} \approx 0.930.
\end{equation}

The ratio of effective holding times $720 / 40 = 18$ closely matches the physical ratio
of traversal times $36 / 2 = 18$. Beyond $\mu=20$, self-loops become so strong that
convergence slows significantly and returns diminish.

\subsection{The Two-Phase Structure as Absorption}

The $2N \times 2N$ block matrix with the zero bottom-left block is an \emph{absorbing
Markov chain}: once in the Down phase, a walker can never return to the Up phase. The
Down phase is therefore a \emph{closed communicating class}.

At the stationary distribution, all mass would eventually be absorbed into the Down phase
in the pure transition matrix. The PageRank teleportation (with probability $1-d$)
continuously injects mass back into the Up phase, preventing full absorption and
sustaining a nontrivial Up-phase stationary distribution.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation of the Two Phases}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Up phase} captures the ``in-transit'' traffic: vehicles moving toward
    their destinations, accumulating on high-throughput arteries according to the
    self-loop dynamics.
  \item \textbf{Down phase} captures ``at-destination'' traffic: vehicles in residential
    streets, parking lots, and local business districts---explained by the
    neighbourhood-level dispersal that $P$ provides.
\end{itemize}

The sum $\vf = \vup^* + \vdown^*$ aggregates both contributions, giving a complete
picture of where vehicles are at any given moment.

    $d$ & Damping factor & 0.80 & Probability of following transition (vs.\ teleporting)\\
    $\gamma$ & Smoothing factor & 0.26 & Graph-diffusion smoothing strength\\
    $\alpha$ & Laplace prior & 0.10 & Pseudo-count added to raw observations\\
    $\varepsilon$ & Convergence tol. & $10^{-6}$ & Power iteration stopping criterion (L1)\\
    \bottomrule

The full algorithmic pipeline can be written compactly as:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{c}_{\text{smooth}} &= (1-\gamma)\,(\mathbf{c}+\alpha\mathbf{1})
    + \gamma\,\mathbf{P}_{\text{adj}}\,(\mathbf{c}+\alpha\mathbf{1}) \tag{Smooth}\\
  E_N &= \frac{\mathbf{c}_{\text{smooth}}}{\norm{\mathbf{c}_{\text{smooth}}}_1}
    \tag{Normalise}\\
  \sw_i &= \mu \cdot \ell_i / s_i, \quad
    P_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij}}{\sw_i + \sum_k w_{ik}} \tag{Build $P$}\\
  M_{2N} &= \begin{bmatrix}(1-\beta)P & \beta I\\ \mathbf{0} & P\end{bmatrix},
    \quad E_{2N} = \begin{bmatrix}E_N\\\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix} \tag{Build $M_{2N}$}\\
  v^* &= \lim_{k\to\infty}
    \left(d\,M_{2N}^\top\right)^k v^{(0)} + (1-d)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
    \left(d\,M_{2N}^\top\right)^k E_{2N} \tag{Stationary dist.}\\
  \vf &= \frac{v^*[1:N] + v^*[N+1:2N]}{\norm{v^*[1:N] + v^*[N+1:2N]}_1} \tag{Final}
\end{align}

\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================

The Two-Phase PageRank with Travel-Time Self-Loops resolves the core deficiency of
applying discrete-time random walks to physical road networks. The key contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Physical dwell time} ($\mu\!=\!20$): A single interpretable parameter
    transforms the transition matrix into a CTMC approximation, allowing realistic traffic
    concentration to emerge naturally from network topology and link dimensions.

  \item \textbf{Two-phase structure} ($\beta\!=\!0.90$): Separating active driving from
    local dispersal prevents forward-phase over-diffusion and correctly models
    where vehicles are parked versus in-transit.

  \item \textbf{Graph-diffusion pre-processing} ($\gamma\!=\!0.26$): Cross-validated
    spatial smoothing converts the sparse GPS sample into a continuous probability field,
    eliminating numerical singularities and providing a statistically principled
    teleportation target.

  \item \textbf{Simultaneous dual improvement}: The model achieves a \textbf{95.7\%}
    reduction in Top-100 MRE and a \textbf{76.2\%} reduction in Overall MRE
    \emph{simultaneously}, demonstrating the fix is physically principled and not
    over-fitted to a single metric.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{result}
  By injecting physical reality into the mathematics---traversal time, phase transitions,
  and spatial smoothing---the algorithm transforms an inaccurate network-flow model into
  a state-of-the-art traffic prediction system without any machine-learning machinery.
\end{result}
